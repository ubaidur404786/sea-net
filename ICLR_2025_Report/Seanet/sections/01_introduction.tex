\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{sec:intro:problem}

A time series is a sequence of measurements taken in time order, and \emph{time series
classification} (TSC) is the task of assigning one label to a complete sequence: normal or abnormal
for a heartbeat recording, healthy or faulty for a machine sensor, one traffic pattern or another
for a web server log. Deep learning models solve this task well, and convolutional architectures in
particular are its standard baselines. Accuracy alone, however, is not enough in the applications
that motivate this work, which impose three requirements \emph{at the same time}:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Accurate classification.} The model must remain competitive with the strong
        convolutional baselines used in the TSC literature.
  \item \textbf{Interpretable predictions.} The model must indicate \emph{which time steps} caused
        its decision, and that indication must be the quantity the decision was actually computed
        from, not a reconstruction produced afterwards by a separate tool.
  \item \textbf{A small parameter budget.} The model must be small enough to be a realistic
        candidate for deployment on an edge device or a microcontroller unit (MCU), where memory is
        measured in hundreds of kilobytes.
\end{enumerate}

Each requirement is well studied on its own. The difficulty, and the subject of this internship, is
that satisfying all three together normally means trading one against the others: accurate models
tend to be large, and small models tend to be black boxes.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:motivation}

Time series are produced continuously by sensors close to where the data is generated: medical
monitors, industrial equipment, network probes. Sending every measurement to a server is often
impossible or undesirable because of bandwidth, latency, energy or privacy, so the classifier has to
run on the device itself. The MLPerf Tiny benchmark \citep{banbury2021mlperftiny}, which defines
reference workloads for this setting, uses models of roughly 38.6\,K parameters -- a useful order of
magnitude for what ``small enough'' means here.

Interpretability matters in the same applications for a practical reason. When a model flags an
anomaly in a patient recording or in a machine's vibration signal, the person who must act on that
alert needs to know which part of the signal triggered it, both to trust the alert and to decide
what to do about it. \citet{rudin2019stop} argues that in high-stakes settings a model that is
interpretable \emph{by design} should be preferred to a black box paired with an after-the-fact
explanation, because only the first guarantees that the explanation and the decision are the same
computation.

\subsection{State of the art}
\label{sec:intro:sota}

\paragraph{Deep learning for time series classification.}
\citet{wang2017fcn} showed that two simple convolutional networks -- a Fully Convolutional Network
(FCN) and a residual network (ResNet) -- are strong baselines for TSC and they remain reference
points today. InceptionTime \citep{fawaz2020inceptiontime} extends this line by applying filters of
several kernel sizes in parallel inside one module, capturing patterns of different durations
without choosing a scale in advance; it is the most accurate model of this family and the backbone
the baseline of this work builds on. Temporal Convolutional Networks \citep{bai2018tcn} contribute
the second ingredient reused here, \emph{dilated} convolutions, which widen the region a filter sees
without adding weights. \citet{fawaz2019review} survey the family and confirm that these models
dominate on the \ucr{} archive \citep{dau2019ucr}. All of them share one property that matters here:
they end with global average pooling, which collapses the time axis into a single vector and
destroys the per-time-step information an explanation would need.

\paragraph{Post-hoc explanation methods.}
Because of that limitation, such a model is usually explained by a method applied after training,
on the frozen model. LIME \citep{ribeiro2016lime} fits a simple readable model, for example a sparse
linear one, to the black box's behaviour in a small neighbourhood around one input, and reads each
feature's importance off that surrogate. SHAP \citep{lundberg2017shap} assigns each feature a
contribution derived from Shapley values in cooperative game theory, giving the attribution a set of
formal properties. Saliency methods, of which Integrated Gradients \citep{sundararajan2017ig} is a
widely used representative, use the gradient of the output with respect to the input to score how
sensitive the prediction is to each position. All three are \emph{post-hoc}: separate computations
on top of a trained model, adding inference cost, and returning an approximation of the model that
can disagree with what the model actually computed. That last point is the argument of
\citet{rudin2019stop} and the reason this work follows the interpretable-by-design route.

\paragraph{Interpretable-by-design models for time series.}
Two directions exist. The first goes through \emph{shapelets}, short representative subsequences
whose presence explains the class: VQShape \citep{wen2024vqshape} learns a codebook of abstracted
shapes and represents a series as tokens drawn from it, so a prediction reads as a set of recognised
shapes, while InterpGN \citep{wen2025interpgn} combines a shapelet expert with a deep expert through
a confidence gate. The second direction, the one this report follows, uses Multiple Instance
Learning (MIL). \millet{} \citep{early2024millet} treats a series as a bag of time steps and replaces
global average pooling with a MIL pooling head that scores every time step; the class prediction is
computed from those scores, so they are simultaneously the prediction mechanism and the explanation
and no second computation is needed. \millet{} is the baseline of this work and is described in
\Cref{sec:bg:millet}. Its pooling head descends from attention-based MIL \citep{ilse2018attention},
developed for histopathology images -- a literature this report also borrows from
(\Cref{sec:bg:mil}).

\paragraph{Small models for edge deployment.}
Depthwise-separable convolutions \citep{howard2017mobilenets, chollet2017xception} reduce the cost
of a convolution by splitting it into a per-channel filtering step and a channel-mixing step, and
are the standard tool for building small convolutional networks. MLPerf Tiny
\citep{banbury2021mlperftiny} provides reference models and tasks for microcontroller-class
hardware, and post-training quantisation \citep{jacob2018quantization} shrinks a trained model
further by storing and computing its weights in low-precision integers. These works make small
models accurate, but none of them makes them interpretable.

\paragraph{The gap.}
Putting the two lines side by side gives the problem this internship addresses. \millet{} provides
classification \emph{and} a faithful built-in explanation, but its InceptionTime backbone needs about
424\,K parameters; the edge-oriented models are small enough for a microcontroller but output a class
label only. No existing model delivers all three requirements of \Cref{sec:intro:problem} at once.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contrib}

To close that gap I designed \seanet{}, an inherently interpretable architecture for time series
classification proposed in this report and not a pre-existing model. The name stands for
\emph{Selective Evidence Aggregation Network}, because it combines multi-scale temporal feature
extraction with selective, class-specific aggregation of the evidence found at each time step. It
has two independently replaceable parts -- a \seanet{} \emph{encoder} and a MIL \emph{pooling head}
-- and my contributions cover both, plus the infrastructure needed to compare them:

\begin{contribution}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{The \seanet{} encoder and its variants.} I designed a compact encoder built from
        multi-scale depthwise-separable dilated convolutional blocks, and seven \seanet{} encoder
        variants on top of it: bottleneck, gated, input-gated, spike/trend, reconstruction-residual,
        and two wrappers (multi-scale channels and multi-scale pyramid). Every variant preserves the
        one embedding per time step that an inherent explanation requires
        (\Cref{sec:methods:encoder}).

  \item \textbf{Seven new MIL pooling heads.} I designed and implemented seven MIL pooling
        variants -- class-wise conjunctive, softmax conjunctive with a learnable sharpness
        parameter, adaptive class-wise, Top-$k$ conjunctive, attention-max, per-class gated
        attention and dual-stream conjunctive. Each one targets a specific limitation of the
        pooling head used by \millet{}, and five of them provably reduce to that baseline for one
        setting of their parameters (\Cref{sec:methods:pooling}).

  \item \textbf{A modular, configuration-driven benchmark.} I built a pipeline in which any encoder
        can be paired with any pooling head from a single configuration file, with resumable
        multi-dataset runs, experiment tracking, and automatic regeneration of every figure and
        table in this report from the stored results (\Cref{sec:setup:pipeline}).

  \item \textbf{An empirical study of 72 encoder and pooling-head combinations} over \webtraffic{}
        and the \ucr{} archive, measuring accuracy, explanation quality and model cost together,
        against FCN, ResNet and \millet{} baselines that I re-trained myself under an identical
        training configuration (\Cref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}
\end{contribution}
