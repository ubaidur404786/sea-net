\subsection{Time series classification}
\label{sec:bg:tsc}

A univariate time series of length $\nsteps$ is just a list of numbers in time order,
$\bag = (\inst_1, \dots, \inst_{\nsteps})$. Time series classification (TSC) means giving the whole
list one label $y \in \{1, \dots, \nclz\}$ -- for example ``this heartbeat is normal''. What happens
at one point usually depends most on what happened just before and just after it, so a convolution
filter fits well: it only looks at a small window around each point, called the \emph{receptive
field}. A plain stack of convolutions needs many layers before it can see far back, so Temporal
Convolutional Networks (TCNs) \citep{bai2018tcn} use \emph{dilated} convolutions, where the filter
skips over some points and the window grows quickly for almost no extra cost. My encoder
(\Cref{sec:architecture}) is built on this idea, together with depthwise-separable convolutions
\citep{chollet2017xception, howard2017mobilenets}: instead of one convolution that mixes time and
channels at once, a cheap step filters each channel on its own and a cheap $1\times1$ step mixes the
channels, which needs far fewer weights for almost the same result.

Three deep networks are the standard comparison point, and I use all three as baselines: FCN and
ResNet \citep{wang2017fcn}, and InceptionTime \citep{fawaz2020inceptiontime}, which runs several
kernel sizes in parallel so the model does not have to guess one ``right'' kernel size. For a full
survey of this family the review of \citet{fawaz2019review} is the place to read. What matters here
is one property all three share: they end with global average pooling, which averages every time
step into a single vector before the classifier. That step throws away exactly the information
needed to say \emph{which} time step mattered, which is why none of them can explain its own
prediction.

\subsection{Multiple instance learning}
\label{sec:bg:mil}

In multiple instance learning (MIL) \citep{dietterich1997mil}, the \emph{bag} carries the label and
the \emph{instances} inside it do not. Applied to a time series, the bag is the whole series and
each time step is one instance.

A simple picture helps here. Think of a key ring with many keys. The ring opens the door if at
least one key on it fits the lock. You are told the ring works, but never which key was the one
that fitted. That is exactly a bag label being known while the instance labels stay hidden. It also
explains why MIL suits time series so well: a series is labelled as a whole, but usually only a few
time steps really carry the evidence for that label.

A \emph{pooling function} is the rule that turns the $\nsteps$ instance embeddings of a bag into one
bag embedding. The two simplest rules are too rigid. Mean pooling treats every time step as equally
important. Max pooling keeps only the single strongest one and ignores everything else.
Attention-based MIL \citep{ilse2018attention} fixes this by \emph{learning} a weight for every
instance instead of fixing the rule in advance: a small neural network looks at each instance and
outputs a number saying how much it should count, and the bag embedding is the weighted average.

Because the network produces those weights itself, they can be read directly as an importance score
for every instance. This is the property that \millet{} and every pooling head in this report reuse
to get an explanation for free.

\subsection{The \millet{} framework}
\label{sec:bg:millet}

\begin{priorwork}
Everything in this subsection is the work of \citet{early2024millet}. I reuse their formulation,
their metric implementation and their data loaders without change. My own heads in
\Cref{sec:pooling} are written in the same notation, so the two can be compared line by line.
\end{priorwork}

\paragraph{General form.}
An encoder $f_\theta$ maps the series to one embedding per time step,
\begin{equation}
  \emb_j = f_\theta(\bag)_j \in \R^{\dmodel},
  \qquad j = 1, \dots, \nsteps ,
  \label{eq:encoder_generic}
\end{equation}
and a MIL pooling head turns these $\nsteps$ embeddings into a bag prediction
$\bagpred \in \R^{\nclz}$ together with an interpretation
$\interp \in \R^{\nclz \times \nsteps}$, where $\interp_{k,j}$ says how much time step $j$ pushed
the model towards class $k$.

\paragraph{Conjunctive pooling (the baseline).}
The head used by the \millet{} baseline combines one attention gate with one per-time-step
classifier:
\begin{align}
  a_j       &= \sig\!\left(\mathbf{w}_a^\top \tanh(W_a \emb_j)\right) \in [0,1],
  \label{eq:conj_gate}\\
  \instpred_j &= W_c \emb_j + \mathbf{b}_c \in \R^{\nclz},
  \label{eq:conj_inst}\\
  \bagpred^{\,k} &= \frac{1}{\nsteps} \sum_{j=1}^{\nsteps} a_j\, \instpred_j^{\,k},
  \qquad
  \interp_{k,j} = a_j\, \instpred_j^{\,k}.
  \label{eq:conj_bag}
\end{align}

In words: the gate $a_j$ in \Cref{eq:conj_gate} answers ``how much should the model listen to time
step $j$'', and the classifier $\instpred_j$ in \Cref{eq:conj_inst} answers ``what does time step
$j$ vote for''. \Cref{eq:conj_bag} then builds the class score as the average, over every time step,
of that vote weighted by how much attention the step received.

The important part is that the interpretation $\interp_{k,j}$ is made of the exact same numbers that
build $\bagpred^{\,k}$. So the explanation can never disagree with the prediction. It is not
computed afterwards by a separate method -- it is read directly off the sum that produced the class
score.

\paragraph{The three weak points I build on.}
Each weakness below becomes one of my pooling heads in \Cref{sec:pooling}, in the same order.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{One gate for all classes.} The gate $a_j$ is a single number shared by every class,
        so a time step cannot support class A and argue against class B at the same time.
  \item \textbf{The weights are not normalised.} The gate is a sigmoid and the sum is divided by
        $\nsteps$, so the scale of the bag score depends on how long the series is. Two series with
        the same evidence but different lengths do not get the same score.
  \item \textbf{The aggregator is a fixed mean.} On a long series, a handful of decisive points get
        diluted by hundreds of background points, and the mean cannot do anything about it.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Measuring the quality of an explanation}
\label{sec:bg:metrics}

\paragraph{AOPCR: does removing the ``important'' points really hurt the model?}
A picture alone cannot prove that an explanation is correct, because a heatmap can look convincing
even when the model ignored it. AOPC (Area Over the Perturbation Curve) \citep{samek2017aopc} tests
it directly: rank the time steps from most to least important according to the model's own
interpretation, then mask them one at a time, most important first, and watch how fast the model's
confidence in its own prediction falls. A faithful explanation should cause a fast drop, because
removing the points it called important should really hurt. \millet{} \citep{early2024millet} adds
a control to get AOPCR: the same masking is repeated in a random order, and the score reports how
much better the real ranking does than chance.

Write $\bagpred^{k}(\bag)$ for the model's confidence in class $k$ on the series $\bag$, and let
$O^k$ be the time steps sorted from most to least important according to $\interp_{k,\cdot}$. For
$j = 1,\dots,\nsteps-1$, let $\bag^{O^k}_{j}$ be the same series with the $j$ most important steps
masked out. Then
\begin{equation}
  \text{AOPC}(\bag, O^k) \;=\; \frac{1}{\nsteps-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\nsteps-1}
  \Big[\bagpred^{k}(\bag) - \bagpred^{k}\big(\bag^{O^k}_{j}\big)\Big] ,
  \label{eq:aopc}
\end{equation}
and AOPCR repeats \Cref{eq:aopc} with three random orderings $R^{(1)}, R^{(2)}, R^{(3)}$ and reports
how much the real ranking beats the average random one:
\begin{equation}
  \text{AOPCR}(\bag, O^k) \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\sum_{r=1}^{3}
  \Big[\text{AOPC}(\bag, O^k) - \text{AOPC}\big(\bag, R^{(r)}\big)\Big] .
  \label{eq:aopcr}
\end{equation}

A higher AOPCR means the model's confidence drops faster when the ranked evidence is removed than
when random points are removed, so the ranking really points at evidence and not at noise.

One warning is needed, and it matters for how this report should be read. AOPCR is \textbf{not
normalised}: it is built from the model's raw confidence, so its scale follows the scale of that
model's logits. My AOPCR values are safe to compare with \emph{each other}, because every model here
is trained the same way, but not against an AOPCR printed in another paper trained differently. I
show direct evidence for this in \Cref{sec:disc:reading}.

\paragraph{NDCG@$n$: did the model rank the truly modified steps at the top?}
\webtraffic{} is special because, for every series, we know exactly which time steps the generator
changed to create the event the model must classify. None of the other datasets in this report have
that per-time-step ground truth, so this check is only possible there. NDCG@$n$
\citep{jarvelin2002ndcg} is the standard score for exactly this question: given a ranked list, do
the items that truly matter appear near the top?
\begin{equation}
  \text{NDCG@}n(O^k) \;=\; \frac{1}{\text{IDCG@}n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
  \frac{\text{rel}_j}{\log_2(j+1)} ,
  \label{eq:ndcg}
\end{equation}
where $\text{rel}_j \in \{0,1\}$ is $1$ if the time step ranked $j$-th is truly one of the modified
steps, and $0$ otherwise. $\text{IDCG@}n$ is the same sum on the best possible ranking, so
$\text{NDCG@}n \in [0,1]$ and $1.0$ is perfect.

Unlike AOPCR, NDCG@$n$ is normalised and is checked against a real answer, which is why I lean on it
more than on AOPCR whenever the two disagree.

\subsection{Related work in one paragraph}
\label{sec:bg:related}

Post-hoc explainers such as LIME \citep{ribeiro2016lime}, SHAP \citep{lundberg2017shap} and
Integrated Gradients \citep{sundararajan2017ig} are applied \emph{after} a model is trained and
frozen, so the separate explanation they build can disagree with what the model actually computed.
\citet{rudin2019stop} argues that for high-stakes decisions it is safer to use a model that is
interpretable \emph{by design}, which is the position \millet{} takes and the one this report takes
too. Other interpretable-by-design TSC models go through shapelets rather than MIL
\citep{wen2025interpgn, wen2024vqshape}. MIL itself is used heavily outside time series, most of all
on whole-slide pathology images, where one slide is a bag and one image patch is an instance
\citep{ilse2018attention, hashimoto2020msdamil, li2021dsmil}; two of my pooling heads
(\Cref{sec:pooling}) adapt ideas from that literature. So this report sits between the two lines: it
keeps \millet{}'s built-in, always-faithful explanation and borrows pooling ideas from
pathology-image MIL to fix the three weaknesses listed above.
