\subsection{Time series classification}
\label{sec:bg:tsc}

\begin{guide}
Half a page. Define the task, then name the three standard deep backbones that this
work compares against, and say in one line what each does.
\textbf{Questions to answer:} What makes time series different from images or text?
Why do convolutions work on them? What is a \emph{receptive field} and why does it
matter for long series?
\end{guide}

\todo{Definition paragraph.} A univariate time series of length $\nsteps$ is a vector
$\bag = (\inst_1, \dots, \inst_{\nsteps})$, and time series classification (TSC) means
predicting one label $y \in \{1, \dots, \nclz\}$ for the whole series.

\todo{One paragraph on the standard deep baselines: FCN and ResNet
\citep{wang2017fcn}, InceptionTime \citep{fawaz2020inceptiontime}, and the general
picture given by the review of \citet{fawaz2019review}. One sentence each. Say that
all three end with global average pooling, which is exactly the step that destroys
the per-time-step information.}
{Three deep networks are the standard comparison point in the TSC literature. The
Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) and the Residual Network (ResNet) of
\citet{wang2017fcn} were the first strong deep baselines: they replace hand-picked
features with a stack of 1-D convolutions, and ResNet adds skip connections so a
deeper stack still trains well. InceptionTime \citep{fawaz2020inceptiontime} goes
further and runs several convolution kernel sizes in parallel inside every block, so
the model does not need to guess one ``right'' kernel size in advance; the review of
\citet{fawaz2019review} covers these and many other deep TSC models and finds that
this general recipe wins on most public benchmarks. All three networks share one
property that matters for this report: they end with global average pooling, which
averages the embedding of every time step into a single vector before the final
classifier. This step throws away exactly the information needed to say
\emph{which} time step mattered, which is why none of these three models can explain
its own prediction.}

\todo{One short paragraph on dilated / temporal convolutional networks
\citep{bai2018tcn} and depthwise-separable convolutions
\citep{chollet2017xception, howard2017mobilenets}, because my encoder is built from
them. Keep it to what the reader needs later.}
{A time series differs from an image or a piece of text in one simple way: it has a
single time axis, and what happens at one time step usually depends the most on what
happened just before and after it, not on points far away. A convolution filter is a
natural fit for this, because it only looks at a small, fixed-size window around each
point; this window is called the \emph{receptive field}. Stacking more convolution
layers grows the receptive field, but a plain stack needs many layers to see far back
in a long series. Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) \citep{bai2018tcn} solve this
with \emph{dilated} convolutions: the filter skips over some points, so the receptive
field grows quickly with each extra layer instead of only by a fixed amount, without
adding much compute. My encoder in \Cref{sec:architecture} is built on this idea. It
also uses depthwise-separable convolutions \citep{chollet2017xception,
howard2017mobilenets}, first used to shrink image models for mobile phones: instead of
one convolution that mixes both time and channels at once, the work is split into a
cheap step that filters each channel on its own, followed by a cheap $1\times1$ step
that mixes the channels, which needs far fewer parameters for almost the same
result.}

\subsection{Multiple instance learning}
\label{sec:bg:mil}

\begin{guide}
Half a page. This is the key idea of the whole report, so explain it with a picture
and an everyday example (a key ring: the ring opens the door if \emph{at least one}
key on it fits; you are told the ring works, not which key).
\textbf{Questions to answer:} What is a bag, what is an instance, what is a pooling
function? Why does MIL give a per-instance score for free? Which pooling functions
exist (mean, max, attention) and what are they good at?
\end{guide}

\todo{Definition paragraph.} In multiple instance learning
\citep{dietterich1997mil}, a \emph{bag} carries the label and the \emph{instances}
inside it do not. Applied to a time series, the bag is the whole series and each
time step is one instance.
{A simple everyday picture helps here: think of a key ring with many keys, where the
ring opens the door if \emph{at least one} key on it fits the lock. You are only told
whether the ring works, never which key was the one that fit -- this is exactly like a
bag label being known while the instance labels stay hidden. This picture also
explains why MIL suits time series classification so well: a series is usually
labelled as a whole, while only a handful of time steps actually carry the evidence
for that label.}

\todo{One paragraph on attention-based MIL \citep{ilse2018attention}: the network
learns a weight for each instance, and those weights are readable as importance.}
{A pooling function is the rule that turns the $\nsteps$ instance embeddings of a bag
into one bag embedding. The two simplest rules, mean pooling and max pooling, are
both too rigid: mean pooling treats every time step as equally important, and max
pooling keeps only the single strongest one and ignores the rest. Attention-based MIL
\citep{ilse2018attention} fixes this by \emph{learning} a weight for every instance
instead of fixing the rule in advance. A small neural network looks at each instance
embedding and outputs a number that says how much that instance should count towards
the bag prediction; the bag embedding is then a weighted average using these learned
weights. Because the network produces the weights itself, they can be read directly as
an importance score for every instance -- this is exactly the property that
\millet{} and every pooling head in this report reuse to build an explanation for
free.}

\subsection{The \millet{} framework}
\label{sec:bg:millet}

\begin{guide}
About one page. This is the direct starting point of my work, so be precise, and be
equally precise that it is \emph{not} mine. Present the general model first, then the
Conjunctive head, which is the baseline I try to beat everywhere in this report.
\textbf{Questions to answer:} What does \millet{} change compared with a normal
classifier? Why is the explanation ``free'' and always consistent with the
prediction? What are the weak points I later attack?
\end{guide}

\begin{priorwork}
Everything in this subsection is the work of \citet{early2024millet}. I reuse their
formulation, their metric implementation and their data loaders without change; my
own heads in \Cref{sec:pooling} are written in the same notation so that the two can
be compared line by line.
\end{priorwork}

\paragraph{General form.}
An encoder $f_\theta$ maps the series to one embedding per time step,
\begin{equation}
  \emb_j = f_\theta(\bag)_j \in \R^{\dmodel},
  \qquad j = 1, \dots, \nsteps ,
  \label{eq:encoder_generic}
\end{equation}
and a MIL pooling head turns these $\nsteps$ embeddings into a bag prediction
$\bagpred \in \R^{\nclz}$ together with an interpretation
$\interp \in \R^{\nclz \times \nsteps}$, where $\interp_{k,j}$ says how much time step
$j$ pushed the model towards class $k$.

\paragraph{Conjunctive pooling (the baseline).}
The head used by the \millet{} baseline combines one attention gate with one
per-time-step classifier:
\begin{align}
  a_j       &= \sig\!\left(\mathbf{w}_a^\top \tanh(W_a \emb_j)\right) \in [0,1],
  \label{eq:conj_gate}\\
  \instpred_j &= W_c \emb_j + \mathbf{b}_c \in \R^{\nclz},
  \label{eq:conj_inst}\\
  \bagpred^{\,k} &= \frac{1}{\nsteps} \sum_{j=1}^{\nsteps} a_j\, \instpred_j^{\,k},
  \qquad
  \interp_{k,j} = a_j\, \instpred_j^{\,k}.
  \label{eq:conj_bag}
\end{align}
\todo{Two or three sentences in words: the gate $a_j$ says ``how much should I listen
to this time step'', the classifier $\instpred_j$ says ``what does this time step
argue for'', and the bag score is simply their average over time. Because the label is
\emph{built} from $\interp$, the explanation cannot disagree with the prediction.}
{In words, the gate $a_j$ in \Cref{eq:conj_gate} answers ``how much should the model
listen to time step $j$'', while the classifier $\instpred_j$ in \Cref{eq:conj_inst}
answers ``what does time step $j$ vote for''. \Cref{eq:conj_bag} then builds the bag
score $\bagpred^{\,k}$ as the average, over every time step, of that vote weighted by
how much attention the time step received. Because the interpretation $\interp_{k,j}$
uses the exact same numbers that build $\bagpred^{\,k}$, the explanation can never
disagree with the prediction: it is not computed afterwards by a separate step, it is
read directly off the sum that produced the class score.}

\subsection{Measuring the quality of an explanation}
\label{sec:bg:metrics}

\begin{guide}
Half a page and very important: most readers have never seen AOPCR or NDCG@$n$.
Explain the \emph{idea} first in one sentence, then the formula.
\textbf{Questions to answer:} Why can we not just look at the pictures? What does
AOPCR do to the series and why does that test the explanation? When can NDCG@$n$ be
computed at all (only when per-time-step ground truth exists, i.e.\ \webtraffic{})?
Is a higher AOPCR always better?
\end{guide}

\paragraph{AOPCR (Area Over the Perturbation Curve at Random).}
\todo{Plain-English sentence first: remove the time steps the model called most
important, one by one, and watch how fast the model's own confidence drops; compare
that with removing random time steps.}
{A picture alone cannot prove that a model's explanation is correct, because a
heatmap can look convincing even when the model ignored it while deciding. AOPC
(Area Over the Perturbation Curve) \citep{samek2017aopc} tests this directly: rank
the time steps from most to least important according to the model's own
interpretation $\interp_{k,\cdot}$, then mask them one at a time, most important
first, and watch how fast the model's confidence in its own prediction falls. A
faithful explanation should cause a fast drop, because removing the ``important''
points should really hurt the model. \millet{} \citep{early2024millet} adds one
control to this idea to obtain AOPCR: the same masking is repeated with a random
order instead of the model's ranking, and the score reports how much better the real
ranking does than chance.}

Write $\bagpred^{k}(\bag)$ for the model's confidence in class $k$ when it is given
the series $\bag$, and let $O^k$ be the ordering of the time steps
$1,\dots,\nsteps$ sorted from the most to the least important according to
$\interp_{k,\cdot}$ (Most-Relevant-First). For $j = 1,\dots,\nsteps-1$, let
$\bag^{O^k}_{j}$ be the same series with the $j$ most important time steps of that
ordering masked out. AOPC compares the confidence on the clean series against the
confidence as more and more top-ranked steps are removed,
\begin{equation}
  \text{AOPC}(\bag, O^k) \;=\; \frac{1}{\nsteps-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\nsteps-1}
  \Big[\bagpred^{k}(\bag) - \bagpred^{k}\big(\bag^{O^k}_{j}\big)\Big] ,
  \label{eq:aopc}
\end{equation}
and AOPCR repeats \Cref{eq:aopc} with three random orderings $R^{(1)}, R^{(2)},
R^{(3)}$ instead of $O^k$, then reports how much the real ranking beats the average
random one:
\begin{equation}
  \text{AOPCR}(\bag, O^k) \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\sum_{r=1}^{3}
  \Big[\text{AOPC}(\bag, O^k) - \text{AOPC}\big(\bag, R^{(r)}\big)\Big] .
  \label{eq:aopcr}
\end{equation}
\todo{One sentence: a higher value means the ranking really points at the evidence.
One sentence of warning: AOPCR is not normalised, so its scale follows the scale of
the logits -- values are comparable \emph{between my models}, but not against numbers
printed in another paper trained with a different recipe.}
{A higher AOPCR means the model's own confidence drops faster when the ranked
evidence is removed than when random points are removed, so the ranking really
points at real evidence and not at noise. One warning is needed here: AOPCR is not
normalised to a fixed range, because it is built directly from the model's raw
confidence, so its scale follows the scale of that model's logits. This means AOPCR
values are safe to compare \emph{between my own models}, which are all trained the
same way, but not against a raw AOPCR number printed in another paper that used a
different training recipe.}

\paragraph{NDCG@$n$.}
\todo{Plain-English sentence: on \webtraffic{} we know exactly which time steps were
modified, so we can check whether the model ranked those time steps at the top.
NDCG \citep{jarvelin2002ndcg} is the standard score for ``is the true item near the
top of my ranked list''; 1.0 is perfect.}
{\webtraffic{} is special because, for every series, we know in advance exactly which
time steps were artificially changed to create the event that the model must
classify; none of the other datasets used in this report have this per-time-step
ground truth, so this check is only possible on \webtraffic{}. NDCG@$n$
\citep{jarvelin2002ndcg} is the standard information-retrieval score for exactly this
situation: given a ranked list, it asks whether the items that truly matter appear
near the top of that list.}
\begin{equation}
  \text{NDCG@}n(O^k) \;=\; \frac{1}{\text{IDCG@}n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
  \frac{\text{rel}_j}{\log_2(j+1)} ,
  \label{eq:ndcg}
\end{equation}
where $\text{rel}_j \in \{0,1\}$ is $1$ if the time step ranked $j$-th by $O^k$ (the
$j$-th most important step according to $\interp_{k,\cdot}$) is truly one of the
modified time steps, and $0$ otherwise; $\text{IDCG@}n$ is the same sum computed on
the best possible ranking, so that $\text{NDCG@}n \in [0,1]$, with $1.0$ meaning a
perfect ranking.

  confidence as the highest-ranked time steps are masked, next to the curve obtained
  by masking random time steps. The shaded area between the two curves is the score.}
  \caption{\todo{Caption: reading the AOPCR curve.} The x-axis is the number of

  \label{fig:aopcr_curve}
\end{figure}

\todo{One paragraph. Post-hoc explainers \citep{ribeiro2016lime, lundberg2017shap,
sundararajan2017ig} explain a fixed model afterwards and can disagree with it;
\citet{rudin2019stop} argues for models that are interpretable by design. MIL is
heavily used on whole-slide images, where a slide is a bag and a patch is an instance
\citep{ilse2018attention, hashimoto2020msdamil, li2021dsmil}; two of my pooling heads
adapt ideas from that literature to time series. End with one sentence positioning my
work.}
{Two broad families of methods try to explain a model's decision. Post-hoc
explainers, such as LIME \citep{ribeiro2016lime}, SHAP \citep{lundberg2017shap} and
Integrated Gradients \citep{sundararajan2017ig}, are applied \emph{after} a model is
trained and fixed; because they build a separate, approximate explanation, that
explanation can disagree with what the model actually computed. \citet{rudin2019stop}
argues that, for high-stakes decisions, it is safer to use a model that is
interpretable \emph{by design}, so the explanation and the prediction are guaranteed
to match -- this is exactly the position that \millet{} and this report take.
Multiple instance learning is also heavily used outside time series, most of all on
whole-slide pathology images, where one slide is a bag and one small image patch is
an instance \citep{ilse2018attention, hashimoto2020msdamil, li2021dsmil}; two of my
pooling heads in \Cref{sec:pooling} adapt ideas from that literature to time series.
This report sits between these two lines of work: it keeps \millet{}'s built-in,
always-faithful explanation, while borrowing pooling ideas from the pathology-image
MIL literature to fix the three weaknesses of the Conjunctive baseline.}
