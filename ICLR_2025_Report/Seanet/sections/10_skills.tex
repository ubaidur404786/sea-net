\subsection{Scientific skills}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \todo{Reading a research paper well enough to re-implement it and to find its
        weak points -- \Cref{sec:bg:millet}.}
        {I learned to read a paper closely enough to rebuild it, not just summarise
        it: reproducing \millet{}'s Conjunctive pooling and its AOPCR/NDCG@$n$
        metrics (\Cref{sec:bg:millet}) meant going past the equations in the paper to
        their actual code, and noticing where my honest re-implementation still did
        not land on their published number (\Cref{sec:disc:reading}) -- a gap I could
        only explain once I understood the method well enough to know which knob
        (recipe, not architecture) was the likely cause.}
  \item \todo{Designing a controlled comparison: change one thing at a time, keep the
        recipe fixed, re-train the baselines instead of quoting them.}
        {I learned why quoting a competing method's published number is not enough:
        I re-trained FCN, ResNet and InceptionTime myself with \seanet{}'s exact
        recipe (\Cref{sec:setup:baselines}) so that any difference in
        \Cref{sec:results} could only come from the architecture, not from two
        papers using two different numbers of epochs.}
  \item \todo{Reading a metric critically instead of trusting it (the AOPCR scale
        issue, \Cref{sec:disc:reading}).}
        {I learned not to trust a metric's absolute value just because it is defined
        by a formula: finding that my own re-trained \millet{} model's AOPCR sits at
        a completely different scale from the published one, even though it is the
        identical architecture (\Cref{sec:disc:reading}), taught me to check a
        metric's assumptions (here, that AOPCR is unnormalised) before comparing two
        numbers that use it.}
  \item \todo{Transferring a method between fields: MIL pooling from
        whole-slide-image pathology to time series.}
        {I learned to adapt an idea from a different field rather than copy it
        wholesale: Gated attention and Dual-stream pooling both come from
        whole-slide-image cancer pathology (\Cref{sec:pool:gated},
        \Cref{sec:pool:dsmil}), and getting them to work on time series meant
        re-deriving which parts of the original mechanism depended on the WSI
        setting (a feature extractor over image patches) and which parts were
        general MIL ideas that transfer directly.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Technical skills}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \todo{PyTorch: writing custom modules, keeping a strict input/output contract
        so components stay interchangeable.}
        {I learned to design modules around a fixed contract rather than a fixed
        implementation: every encoder in \texttt{seanet/features.py} maps
        $(B, C, T) \to (B, \dmodel, T)$ and every pooling head in
        \texttt{seanet/pooling.py} maps $(B, \dmodel, T)$ to the same output dict
        (\Cref{sec:architecture}), which is the only reason 66 different
        encoder/pooling combinations (\Cref{sec:setup:pipeline}) could be built and
        trained without writing 66 separate model classes.}
  \item \todo{Experiment engineering: configuration files, a model registry, resumable
        sweeps, atomic result writing, MLflow tracking.}
        {I learned to treat the experiment pipeline itself as something worth
        engineering, not just a script: a config-driven model registry, sweeps that
        resume instead of restart, and one results row per (dataset, model, seed)
        (\Cref{sec:setup:pipeline}) turned out to matter as much to how much I could
        get done as any single modelling idea did.}
  \item \todo{Running jobs on a shared HPC platform (Grid'5000): reserving nodes with
        the scheduler, working with a job time limit, splitting one study over two
        sites.}
        {I learned to work around a shared, time-limited resource rather than
        against it: reserving GPU nodes on Grid'5000 with OAR, planning around each
        job's wall-clock limit, and running \seanet{}'s recipe unchanged on two
        different sites (Lille and Sophia, \Cref{sec:setup:pipeline}) so a delay on
        one site did not stall the whole sweep.}
  \item \todo{Reproducibility: one command regenerates every figure and table in this
        report from the stored results.}
        {I learned to make ``regenerate everything'' a single command
        (\texttt{python main.py paper}, \Cref{app:commands}) rather than a manual
        step, specifically so that no number in this report could quietly go stale
        after a re-run. This turned out to matter for a concrete reason while writing
        \Cref{sec:results}: some of the auto-generated tables had been produced
        before a later re-run of the \millet{} baseline finished, so a couple of
        published-looking numbers were already out of date -- exactly the failure
        mode this command is meant to prevent, and a reminder to re-run it once more
        right before submission.}
  \item \todo{Tooling: Git, remote development over SSH, \LaTeX{}.}
        {I learned the day-to-day tools of a research codebase: Git for tracking a
        project that changed shape several times (\Cref{sec:objectives}), VS Code
        over SSH for developing against Grid'5000 directly, and \LaTeX{} for a report
        long and structured enough that plain text would not have held together.}
\end{itemize}

{If I had to name one lesson, it is that a careful protocol can overturn a conclusion
I had already half-believed. Before I checked the 85-dataset \ucr{} sweep, the
\webtraffic{} numbers alone (\Cref{tab:web_main}) told a clean story: my proposed
heads win on accuracy, AOPCR and NDCG@$n$ at once, for a fraction of the parameters.
Running the same models against the same re-trained baseline over 85 more datasets
(\Cref{sec:res:ucr}) showed that story does not fully hold -- the re-trained baseline
actually generalises better on average. Neither number is wrong; only the second one
is complete. That is the concrete version of ``do not trust a result you only checked
once,'' and it is the reason \Cref{sec:disc:limitations} exists as a real section of
this report rather than a formality at the end.}
