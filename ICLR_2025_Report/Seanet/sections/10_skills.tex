\section{Skills Acquired}
\label{sec:skills}

Scientifically, reproducing \millet{} (\Cref{sec:bg:millet}) meant going past the equations in the
paper and into the authors' code, and then being able to name why my honest re-implementation still
missed their published number -- the training recipe, not the architecture
(\Cref{sec:disc:reading}). The same care shaped the protocol: I re-trained FCN, ResNet and
InceptionTime under \seanet{}'s own recipe (\Cref{sec:setup:baselines}), so any difference in
\Cref{sec:results} could only come from the architecture, and I learned to question a metric before
trusting it after finding that the same model scores AOPCR 2.57 under my recipe and 13.27 under
\millet{}'s. Technically, designing every encoder and head around one fixed tensor contract is the
only reason 72 variants could be trained without writing 72 model classes; running them meant
working on a shared HPC platform, reserving GPU nodes on Grid'5000 with OAR across two sites so a
queue at one did not stall the sweep; and making a single command rebuild every figure and table in
this report from the stored results (\Cref{app:commands}) caught real numbers that had quietly gone
out of date. Professionally, the update I found hardest to prepare was bringing my supervisor a
discrepancy I had not solved (objective~O2) instead of debugging privately until it matched, which
turned a private problem into a shared decision. But the lesson I would keep is the last one: before
the 85-dataset \ucr{} sweep the \webtraffic{} numbers told a clean story, and running the same models
against the same re-trained baseline over 85 more datasets (\Cref{sec:res:ucr}) showed it does not
fully hold. Neither number is wrong; only the second one is complete. That is the concrete version
of ``do not trust a result you only checked once'', and it is why \Cref{sec:disc:limitations} is a
real section of this report and not a formality at the end.
