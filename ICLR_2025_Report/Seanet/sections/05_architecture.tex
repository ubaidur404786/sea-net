\section{Proposed Architecture: \seanet{}}
\label{sec:architecture}

\begin{contribution}
The encoder described in this section is my own design. It replaces the
InceptionTime backbone used by \millet{} while keeping exactly the same interface, so
that any encoder can be paired with any pooling head and the comparison stays fair.
\end{contribution}

\begin{guide}
Structure that works well here: design goals $\rightarrow$ overview picture
$\rightarrow$ one building block in detail $\rightarrow$ the variants I derived from
it $\rightarrow$ why it is small (with the parameter formula).
\textbf{Questions to answer:} Why did I not simply keep InceptionTime? What exactly
does ``multi-scale'' mean here, and why does a time series need it? Why cap the
dilation? Why must the output length stay equal to $\nsteps$?
\end{guide}

\subsection{The multi-scale separable block}
\label{sec:arch:block}

\begin{guide}
The heart of the encoder. Explain in this order: (a) why several kernel sizes at once,
(b) why depthwise-separable, (c) why a residual connection, (d) why the dilation is
capped. One equation, one figure, and words a beginner can follow.
\textbf{Questions to answer:} What does a kernel of size 5 see that a kernel of size
23 does not? What would happen with a single kernel size? What does ``depthwise''
mean, in one sentence?
\end{guide}

Let $\mathbf{h} \in \R^{\dmodel \times \nsteps}$ be the input of a block and $\delta$
its dilation. Write $D_k^{(\delta)}$ for a depthwise convolution of kernel size $k$ and
dilation $\delta$ (one filter per channel), and $P$ for a pointwise $1\times1$
convolution (which mixes the channels). One \emph{unit} is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{U}(\mathbf{h})
  = \operatorname{Drop}\Big(\operatorname{ReLU}\Big(\operatorname{BN}\Big(
      P\Big(\textstyle\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} D_k^{(\delta)}(\mathbf{h})\Big)\Big)\Big)\Big),
  \qquad \mathcal{K} = \{5, 11, 23\},
  \label{eq:unit}
\end{equation}
and one block runs two units and adds the input back:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Block}(\mathbf{h}) = \operatorname{ReLU}\big(\mathbf{h} +
  (\mathcal{U} \circ \mathcal{U})(\mathbf{h})\big).
  \label{eq:block}
\end{equation}
Zero ``same'' padding is used everywhere, so the length $\nsteps$ is unchanged
(goal~G3). Block $i$ uses $\delta_i = \min(2^i, \delta_{\max})$, which grows the
receptive field quickly but keeps it local (goal~G4).

\todo{One short paragraph in words explaining \Cref{eq:unit}: the three kernel sizes
look at the signal at three time scales at once and their outputs are summed;
$1\times1$ then mixes channels; BatchNorm \citep{ioffe2015batchnorm} and ReLU are
standard; dropout regularises.}
{In words, \Cref{eq:unit} looks at the same signal at three time scales at once: a short
kernel of size 5 sees a narrow local pattern, while a kernel of size 23 sees roughly four
times as far, and their outputs are simply added together so the block does not have to
pick one scale in advance. The $1\times1$ pointwise convolution $P$ that follows does not
look at time at all -- it only mixes the $\dmodel$ channels at each time step, combining
what the three kernels found. BatchNorm \citep{ioffe2015batchnorm} keeps the activations on
a stable scale, ReLU adds the non-linearity, and dropout regularises. Because every
convolution in \Cref{eq:unit} is \emph{depthwise} (one filter per channel, \texttt{groups=d}
in the code), this whole step is far cheaper than a normal convolution that mixes channels
and looks at multiple time scales at the same time -- see ``Why this is small'' below for
the exact numbers.}

\paragraph{Why this is small.}
\begin{guide}
Do the arithmetic explicitly -- it is short, it is convincing, and it directly proves
goal~G1. Fill in the numbers for $\dmodel$ and $\mathcal{K}$ from the config.
\end{guide}
A standard convolution with $\dmodel$ input and output channels and kernel size $k$
costs $\dmodel^2 k$ weights. A depthwise-separable pair costs only
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\dmodel k}_{\text{depthwise}} \;+\; \underbrace{\dmodel^2}_{\text{pointwise}}
  \;\ll\; \dmodel^2 k .
  \label{eq:sep_cost}
\end{equation}
\todo{One sentence with the real numbers: with $\dmodel = \num{d}$ and kernels
$\{5,11,23\}$ this gives \num{params} parameters per block instead of \num{params}.}
{Counting the weights in \Cref{eq:unit} directly from the code
(\texttt{seanet/features.py}, \texttt{MultiScaleSepBlock}): with the base width
$\dmodel = 128$ and kernels $\{5,11,23\}$, the three depthwise convolutions together cost
$\dmodel \cdot 6 + \dmodel \cdot 12 + \dmodel \cdot 24 = 5{,}376$ weights (each term includes
one bias per channel), while the pointwise convolution alone costs
$\dmodel^2 + \dmodel = 16{,}512$ -- already three times more than all three depthwise
convolutions combined. With BatchNorm's $2\dmodel = 256$, one unit costs $22{,}144$
parameters, one block (two units, \Cref{eq:block}) costs $44{,}288$, and the full six-block
encoder plus its stem costs about $266{,}752$ parameters. This matches the $269{,}083$
measured for \texttt{seanet\_conjunctive} in \texttt{results/SEA\_NET/leaderboard.csv} (the
small remainder is the pooling head). A standard, non-separable convolution that looked at
the same three kernel sizes would instead cost
$\dmodel^2 \cdot (5{+}11{+}23) = 638{,}976$ weights for that same step -- about $29\times$
more -- which is exactly why goal~G1 is reachable at all.}

\subsection{Encoder variants}
\label{sec:arch:variants}

\begin{guide}

\textbf{Questions to answer:} Which variants were my own idea, and which followed a

\paragraph{Bottleneck.}
\todo{Problem: the pointwise $1\times1$ convolution dominates the parameter count
(it costs $\dmodel^2$).} I factorise it through a narrower rank $r < \dmodel$:
\begin{equation}
  P = P_2 P_1, \qquad P_1 \in \R^{r \times \dmodel}, \quad P_2 \in \R^{\dmodel \times r},
  \qquad \text{cost } \dmodel^2 \rightarrow 2\dmodel r .
  \label{eq:bottleneck}
\end{equation}
\todo{One sentence: with $r = \dmodel/\num{ratio}$ this removes \num{pct}\,\% of the
encoder weights.}
{With the ratio $=4$ used in \texttt{configs/models/sv4/seanet\_bottleneck.yaml}, the
pointwise cost drops from $\dmodel^2$ to $2\dmodel r = \dmodel^2/2$ -- exactly half,
whatever $\dmodel$ is, since $r = \dmodel/4$. Counting the whole encoder at the size
actually used in the experiments ($\dmodel=64$, $4$ blocks): the plain
\texttt{sea\_mstcn\_sep} encoder costs about $56{,}320$ parameters, and the bottleneck
version costs about $40{,}064$ -- a real $29\,\%$ cut in the \emph{full} encoder, not just
the pointwise step, because the pointwise convolution is the single biggest term. This lines
up with the $41{,}324$ measured for \texttt{sea\_mstcn\_sep\_bottleneck} paired with
top-$k$ pooling (\texttt{configs/models/sv6/seanet\_bottleneck\_mschan.yaml}).}

\paragraph{Gated.}
\todo{Problem: not every channel matters for every series.} A summary over time drives
a channel gate:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\mathbf{h}} = \tfrac{1}{\nsteps}\textstyle\sum_j \mathbf{h}_{:,j},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{h}' = \mathbf{h} \odot \sig(W_g \bar{\mathbf{h}}),
  \label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ multiplies element by element. \todo{One sentence on why this helps.}
{This lets the whole-series context decide which feature channels to trust, without
touching individual time steps: the gate multiplies by $(1+\text{gate}) \in [1,2]$ rather
than replacing the features outright (\texttt{seanet/features.py}, \texttt{SummaryGate}), so
the per-time-step differences that AOPCR and NDCG depend on are never flattened -- only the
channels are turned up or down, never the time axis. The code offers three ways to summarise
time into that one vector (max, mean, or the last time step);
\texttt{configs/models/sv4/seanet\_gated\_last.yaml} uses the last time step, kept so all
three could be compared under the same recipe.}

\paragraph{Input gate.}
\todo{Same idea, but the gate is read directly from the raw input instead of from the
deep features. Write the one-line equation and say why I tried both.}
\begin{equation}
  g_j = \sig\!\big(W_g \ast \bag\big)_j \in [0,1]^{\dmodel},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{h}'_j = \mathbf{h}_j \odot (1 + g_j) ,
  \label{eq:input_gate}
\end{equation}
{where $W_g \ast \bag$ is a small convolution (kernel size~7 in
\texttt{configs/models/sv4/seanet\_inputgate.yaml}) applied to the \emph{raw} input series,
not to the encoder's features. The difference from the Gated variant above is exactly this:
\Cref{eq:gate} builds ONE gate for the whole series from the deep features, while
\Cref{eq:input_gate} builds a gate that changes at \emph{every} time step, read straight from
the raw signal (\texttt{seanet/features.py}, \texttt{InputGate}). I tried both because they
attack the same idea -- not every channel matters everywhere -- at two different
granularities, coarse (whole-series) versus fine (per-time-step); comparing them tells us
which granularity the gating idea actually needs.}

\paragraph{Spike / trend.}
\todo{Two branches: a smoothed (trend) branch and a high-pass (spike) branch, mixed
and gated. Say which datasets motivated it.}
\begin{align}
  \text{trend}_j &= \text{mstcn\_sep}(\bag)_j \in \R^{\dmodel} ,
  \label{eq:spike_trend}\\
  \text{spike}_j &= \operatorname{ReLU}\!\big(\operatorname{BN}(\operatorname{Conv1d}_3(
  \bag - \operatorname{avg}_w(\bag)))\big)_j \in \R^{d_{\text{spike}}} ,
  \label{eq:spike_branch}\\
  \mathbf{h}_j &= P_{1\times 1}\big([\text{trend}_j \,;\, \text{spike}_j]\big) \in \R^{\dmodel} ,
  \label{eq:spike_mix}
\end{align}
{followed by the same SummaryGate as the Gated variant. The trend branch is the usual
encoder, unchanged. The spike branch first removes a local moving average $\operatorname{avg}_w$
(window~5 in \texttt{configs/models/sv4/seanet\_spiketrend.yaml}) from the raw series --
what is left is close to zero on smooth stretches and large only where the signal jumps --
and a small convolution turns that high-pass signal into $d_{\text{spike}} = 16$ extra
channels (\texttt{seanet/features.py}, \texttt{SpikeBranch}). The two branches are
concatenated and mixed back to width $\dmodel$ with a $1{\times}1$ convolution.
\note{Add the specific dataset(s) where a single sharp point, rather than a slow trend,
carries the label -- I could not find a confirmed example in the code comments, so check
this against Section 8 before printing a claim.}}

\paragraph{Multi-scale channels \note{wrapper, not an encoder}.}
\todo{Rolling max, min and standard deviation over a window are appended as extra
input channels, so the network gets local statistics for free. Length unchanged.}
\begin{equation}
  \bag^{+} = \Big[\, \bag \,;\; \big(\text{roll\_max}_w(\bag)\big)_{w \in \mathcal{W}} \;;\;
  \big(\text{roll\_min}_w(\bag)\big)_{w \in \mathcal{W}} \;;\;
  \big(\text{roll\_std}_w(\bag)\big)_{w \in \mathcal{W}} \;;\; \operatorname{sign}(\Delta \bag) \,\Big] ,
  \label{eq:mschan}
\end{equation}
{where $\mathcal{W} = \{3,7,15,31\}$ (all odd, so each window stays centred on its time
step) and $\Delta \bag_j = \bag_j - \bag_{j-1}$. This wrapper does not replace an encoder --
it only widens the input from $1$ channel to $1{+}|\mathcal{W}|{\cdot}3{+}1 = 14$ channels
before handing it to any of the encoders above
(\texttt{configs/models/sv6/seanet\_bottleneck\_mschan.yaml} wraps the Bottleneck encoder).
Max, min and standard deviation are used instead of a rolling mean because a mean is a
\emph{linear} operation the stem convolution could already learn on its own, while
max/min/std are non-linear and genuinely add information a convolution cannot compute by
itself. One BatchNorm is applied to these 14 channels before the encoder sees them, because
the built channels do not share the raw series' scale (a rolling max sits high, a rolling
std is small and always positive); skipping this normalisation was tried first and cost
accuracy (\texttt{seanet/features.py}, \texttt{MultiScaleChannelEncoder} docstring). The
series length $\nsteps$ never changes, so goal~G3 still holds.}

\paragraph{Multi-scale pyramid \note{wrapper, not an encoder}.}
\todo{The same encoder runs on the series downsampled by $1,2,4,8$; the outputs are
upsampled back to length $\nsteps$ and fused (attention / mean / max / concat). Write
the fusion in one equation.}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}_j = \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \alpha_j^{s} \, \mathbf{h}_j^{(s)} ,
  \qquad
  \alpha_j^{s} = \operatorname{softmax}_{s}\big(\text{score}(\mathbf{h}_j^{(s)})\big),
  \qquad \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \alpha_j^{s} = 1 ,
  \label{eq:pyramid_fusion}
\end{equation}
{where $\mathcal{S} = \{1,2,4,8\}$ (\texttt{configs/models/sv6/seanet\_bottleneck\_pyramid.yaml}),
$\mathbf{h}_j^{(s)}$ is the SAME shared encoder run on the series averaged down by a factor
$s$ and stretched back to length $\nsteps$, and the softmax normalises over the
\emph{scale} axis, never over time -- so the per-time-step signal AOPCR and NDCG depend on
is untouched (\texttt{seanet/features.py}, \texttt{MultiScalePyramid}). One encoder is
shared across every scale, so the parameter count barely moves; the extra cost is mostly
compute ($1+\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac18 \approx 1.9\times$), not parameters.
\textbf{One constraint made this work at all:} the wrapped encoder must stay shallow enough
that its receptive field does not exceed $\nsteps / \max(\mathcal{S})$, otherwise the
coarsest scales collapse to a near-constant signal and the interpretation degrades badly --
this is why \texttt{configs/models/sv6/seanet\_bottleneck\_pyramid.yaml} uses a shallow
\num{2}-block, \texttt{max\_dilation=1} base instead of the usual \num{4}-block one, and
\texttt{scripts/check\_multiscale.py} checks this automatically for any new scale/base
combination.}

\subsection{Why this design and not another}
