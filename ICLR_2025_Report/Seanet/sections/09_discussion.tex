\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\begin{guide}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What worked}
\label{sec:disc:worked}

\begin{guide}
Three or four short paragraphs, one finding each. Structure every one the same way:
the observation $\rightarrow$ the evidence (point at a table) $\rightarrow$ my
explanation of \emph{why} it happens.
\textbf{Questions to answer:} Which of my design goals (\Cref{sec:arch:goals}) were
actually reached? Which head repaired the weakness it was designed for? Does the
mechanism I claimed in \Cref{sec:pooling} show up in the numbers, or did the head
help for a different reason?
\end{guide}

\todo{Finding 1 -- the size / accuracy trade-off.}

{Goal G1 of \Cref{sec:arch:goals} was a smaller model, and on \webtraffic{} it was
reached without giving up accuracy. \texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} matches or beats
the re-trained \millet{} baseline on accuracy, AOPCR and NDCG@$n$ at once
(\Cref{tab:web_main}) while using 41{,}324 parameters against \millet{}'s 423{,}707 --
a 90\% reduction (\Cref{tab:cost}). This is not a lucky single point either:
\texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk} reaches the single best accuracy in the whole
66-model sweep (0.954) at 61{,}740 parameters, 4.4$\times$ smaller than the
same-accuracy \millet{}-encoder model (\Cref{fig:pareto}). I read this as the
Bottleneck block (\Cref{sec:arch:variants}) and the multi-scale depthwise-separable
design (\Cref{sec:architecture}) doing what they were built to do: most of a
standard-convolution encoder's parameters go into channel mixing, not into the
temporal receptive field, so a pointwise-reduced, depthwise-separable design can keep
the receptive field and drop the channel-mixing cost -- and the accuracy numbers show
that cost was mostly redundant, not load-bearing.}

\todo{Finding 2 -- explanation quality.}

{Goal G2, sharper explanations without a separate mechanism, also holds up:
\texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk}'s NDCG@$n$ (0.773) is the best of the three
\webtraffic{} rows I have full seed data for (\Cref{tab:explanation_quality}), and it
gets there with a mechanism I can point to directly: Top-$k$ pooling averages only the
$\kappa T$ most decisive time steps (\Cref{sec:pool:topk}), so $\Phi_{k,j}$ is
literally zero everywhere the model did not use as evidence, rather than a smoothed
attention weight that is never exactly zero. That is the mechanism
\Cref{sec:pooling} claims, and it shows up in the number without needing a separate
explanation for why NDCG improved. The one place this finding needs a caveat is
\texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive}: it has the best \emph{mean} AOPCR of the three,
but I already showed in \Cref{sec:res:explanations} that the gap over
\texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} is smaller than its own seed-to-seed std, so I count
Top-$k$'s NDCG win as the real, reliable result here and the AOPCR difference as not
yet established.}

\todo{Finding 3 -- which encoder $\times$ head pairing wins and why.}

{The best single model in the whole sweep, \texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk} (Gated
encoder + Top-$k$ pooling, 0.954 accuracy), is not the pairing I would have guessed
from either component's marginal performance: the Gated encoder alone averages 0.902
across its pairings and Top-$k$ pooling alone averages 0.904 -- both comfortably
mid-table, not the best of their kind (\Cref{sec:res:ablation}). Two mediocre-looking
marginals combining into the single best result is, on its own, evidence of a real
interaction rather than either component simply being ``good'': my reading is that the
Gated encoder's per-scale gate (\Cref{sec:arch:variants}) sharpens \emph{which} time
steps carry usable signal before pooling ever sees them, and Top-$k$'s hard selection
is exactly the pooling head best placed to exploit an encoder that has already done
that sharpening -- a smoother head like Softmax+temperature would partly undo the
gate's work by blending in neighbours it just suppressed. I say ``my reading'' and not
``the reason'' deliberately: \Cref{fig:ablation_grid} shows the effect, but I have not
run the controlled ablation (Gated encoder with each pooling head, one factor at a
time) that would confirm this mechanism rather than another one.}

\subsection{What did not work}
\label{sec:disc:negative}

\begin{guide}
A real section, not an apology. Every honest study has these, and reporting them is
part of the scientific method.
\textbf{Questions to answer:} Which of my ideas looked good on paper and did not pay
off? Do I know why, or is it still open? What would I test next to find out?
\end{guide}

\todo{Negative result 1 -- e.g. a head that generalises the baseline but does not beat
it in practice. Say what the theory promised (\Cref{prop:ad} or similar) and why the
promise did not materialise: optimisation difficulty, not enough data, the weakness it
fixes was not the binding one.}

{Dual-stream conjunctive pooling (\Cref{sec:pool:dsmil}) is the clearest negative
result in the sweep. Property~\ref{prop:ds} (``safe by construction'') says it should
never do worse than the Conjunctive baseline, because its blend $\lambda$ can always
be driven to 0 and recover Conjunctive exactly. In practice it is the
\emph{worst}-performing pooling head I tried, by a wide margin: mean \webtraffic{}
accuracy 0.744 across its four encoder pairings, against 0.90+ for Class-wise,
Softmax+temperature and Top-$k$ conjunctive (\Cref{sec:res:ablation}), and one pairing
(Spike/trend encoder) collapses to 0.556. The safety net is real on paper but was not
exercised in training: nothing in the loss pushes $\lambda$ towards 0 specifically, so
if the critical-instance stream (\Cref{sec:pool:dsmil}, Stream~B) learns a bad
argmax-based query early, gradient descent has no reason to fall back to the safe
mean -- an optimisation-difficulty failure, not a capacity or data one, and consistent
with $\lambda$-blended heads in general being harder to train the more their two
streams disagree.}

\todo{Negative result 2.}

{Per-class Gated attention (\Cref{sec:pool:gated}) is a milder version of the same
story. Unlike every other head in \Cref{sec:pooling}, it has no blend parameter and so
cannot reduce to Conjunctive at any setting of its weights (\Cref{sec:pool:gated}) --
I flagged this as a structural difference when I wrote that section, and the results
confirm it matters: its mean \webtraffic{} accuracy (0.918) is respectable but never
the best pairing for any encoder it was tried with (\Cref{sec:res:ablation}), unlike
Top-$k$ or Class-wise conjunctive, which each produce at least one top-10 model. The
two-branch gate ($\tanh$ proposes, $\sigma$ gates, \Cref{sec:pool:gated}) is a
reasonable idea transferred from whole-slide-image pathology, but without a safety net
back to a known-good baseline, it has no floor to fall back on if the gate is
mis-trained -- which I think is why it lands consistently in the middle of the table
rather than either winning or failing outright.}

\todo{One sentence on what these negative results teach: extra capacity is only useful
if training can find it.}

{Both results point at the same lesson: a mechanism that is safe or expressive
\emph{on paper} only pays off if the training recipe actually reaches the regime where
that safety or expressiveness matters, and neither of my two negative heads (nor
Gated attention's un-netted design) got there with the single, generic recipe used for
the whole sweep (\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}).}

\subsection{How the numbers should be read}
\label{sec:disc:reading}

\begin{guide}
This subsection protects my own credibility. Two points must be made clearly:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item AOPCR is not normalised, so its scale follows the scale of the logits. My
        values must be compared with each other, never with the published column.
        The proof that this is a measurement-scale effect and not a model effect is
        that my \emph{re-trained} InceptionTime shows the same shift.
  \item Any difference smaller than the seed-to-seed standard deviation is not a
        result. Say what that spread is, and refuse to claim wins inside it.
\end{itemize}
\end{guide}

\todo{Paragraph 1 -- the AOPCR scale issue, with the re-trained baseline as evidence.}

{AOPCR is not normalised (\Cref{sec:setup:metrics}): it is an area under a curve of
raw logit drops, so its scale follows whatever scale the model's own logits happen to
sit at, not a fixed 0--1 range like accuracy. This is a measurement-scale effect, not
a sign that one model's explanations are categorically different from another's, and
the clearest evidence is my own re-trained \millet{} baseline: it is the
\emph{identical} architecture and pooling head as the published model, yet its AOPCR
(2.569, \Cref{tab:web_main}) sits nowhere near the published 4.5532
(\Cref{tab:ucr_accuracy} -- note this published figure is a \ucr{} number, not a
\webtraffic{} one; see the correction note in \Cref{sec:results}). Same method, same
architecture, different scale -- exactly what a measurement-scale effect looks like,
and exactly why \Cref{sec:setup:metrics} warns against reading my AOPCR column against
anyone else's.}

\todo{Paragraph 2 -- seed variance and what counts as a real difference.}

{Where I have 3 seeds (\Cref{sec:results}), the spread is not small: accuracy std
ranges from 0.010 (the re-trained \millet{} baseline) to 0.030
(\texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive}), and AOPCR std is proportionally far larger --
0.437 for \texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive}, almost a fifth of its own mean value
(\Cref{tab:web_main}). I used this directly as a threshold in \Cref{sec:res:web} and
\Cref{sec:res:explanations}: \texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive}'s AOPCR edge over
\texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} (2.651 vs.\ 2.621) is smaller than either model's own
std, so I reported it as not distinguishable from noise rather than as a win. The
harder problem is that the 66-model \ucr{} sweep has only 1 seed
(\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}), so I cannot apply the same test there -- every single-seed
number in \Cref{sec:res:ucr} could plausibly move by a similar amount on a different
seed, and I have no measurement of how much. That is a real limitation of the study,
not just of this paragraph, and it means the \ucr{}-85 ranking should be read as
indicative, not exact.}

\todo{Paragraph 3 -- any difference between my training budget and the published one
(number of repeats, epochs), and how that shifts the comparison.}

{My recipe trains for at most 400 epochs with patience 60 (\Cref{sec:setup:protocol});
the published \millet{} numbers come from their own recipe, which
\texttt{configs/models/sv1/millet\_paper.yaml} documents as up to 1500 epochs, no
weight decay, and no label smoothing -- a training budget roughly 4$\times$ longer
than mine, with a different regularisation profile. I built that file specifically to
measure the size of this gap by reproducing their exact recipe on my own harness, but
I have not yet run it (it is flagged in that file as slow -- roughly 4$\times$ the
cost of my usual sweep, over 85 datasets), so I cannot yet say how much of the
difference between my re-trained \millet{} baseline and the published numbers is
architecture versus training budget. Until that run exists, any comparison against the
published column should be read as ``different recipe, not directly comparable''
(\Cref{tab:ucr_accuracy}), not as a controlled result.}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}

\begin{guide}
Bullet list, three to five items, one line each. Be specific: ``synthetic data'' is
weak, ``\webtraffic{} is synthetic, so the per-time-step ground truth is exact but the
signal is easier than real traffic'' is strong.
\end{guide}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \todo{\webtraffic{} is synthetic; it is the only dataset with per-time-step
        ground truth, so explanation quality is measured on one dataset only.}
        {\webtraffic{} is synthetic and its modified regions are generated, not
        observed; NDCG@$n$ -- the only metric checked against real ground truth in
        this whole study -- is therefore measured on exactly one dataset
        (\Cref{sec:setup:data}), and I do not know how well it would hold on real,
        noisier traffic data.}
  \item \todo{Number of seeds and what that means for the size of a claim.}
        {Only 3 of my 66 model variants have more than 1 seed on \webtraffic{}, and
        the full 129-dataset \ucr{} sweep is single-seed throughout
        (\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}), so most numbers in \Cref{sec:results} carry no
        variance estimate at all; where I do have 3 seeds, the spread was large
        enough to overturn a headline claim (\Cref{sec:disc:reading}), which is a
        reason for caution about the single-seed numbers, not a reason to trust them
        more.}
  \item \todo{Only univariate series were tested, although the architecture supports
        multivariate input.}
        {Every dataset in this study (\webtraffic{} and the \ucr{} archive,
        \Cref{sec:setup:data}) is univariate; the encoder's channel width $\dmodel$
        does not care how many input channels it starts from, so multivariate input
        should work mechanically, but I have not tested it.}
  \item \todo{Some variants were screened on \webtraffic{} alone before the full
        \ucr{} sweep, which biases the selection slightly.}
        {Both headline variants in \Cref{sec:res:web} were chosen because they did
        well on \webtraffic{}, and \Cref{sec:res:ucr} shows directly what that costs:
        on the 85-dataset \ucr{} archive, my re-trained \millet{} baseline actually
        beats both of them on mean accuracy and average rank. A selection made on one
        dataset should not be expected to be the best choice on 85 different ones,
        and it was not.}
  \item \todo{No human study: a high AOPCR means the explanation is faithful to the
        model, not that a person finds it useful.}
        {AOPCR and NDCG@$n$ both measure whether the model's stated explanation
        matches what the model itself actually relied on (\Cref{sec:bg:metrics}) --
        faithfulness, not usefulness. A high score does not mean a person looking at
        the highlighted time steps would find them informative or trustworthy; that
        would need a human study, which this internship did not have time for.}
\end{itemize}
