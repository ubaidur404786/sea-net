\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What worked}
\label{sec:disc:worked}

\paragraph{The size cut is real, and it did not cost accuracy.}
Goal~G1 of \Cref{sec:arch:goals} was a smaller model. On \webtraffic{} I reached it without giving
anything up. \texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} beats the re-trained \millet{} baseline on accuracy,
AOPCR and NDCG@$n$ at the same time (\Cref{tab:web_main}) while using 41{,}324 parameters against
423{,}707 -- a 90\,\% cut, and 11$\times$ fewer FLOPs (\Cref{tab:cost}). This is not one lucky
point either: \texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk} reaches the best accuracy of the whole 72-model
sweep (0.955) at 61{,}740 parameters.

My explanation is the one the design was built on. In a normal convolution most of the weights go
into mixing channels, not into looking further back in time. The depthwise-separable block
(\Cref{sec:arch:block}) separates those two jobs and the bottleneck (\Cref{eq:bottleneck}) shrinks
the channel-mixing part directly. The accuracy numbers then tell me something I did not know in
advance: most of that channel-mixing capacity was not doing useful work.

\paragraph{Top-$k$ pooling improved the explanation for the reason I claimed.}
\texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} has the best NDCG@$n$ of every model with full seed data
(0.773, \Cref{tab:explanation_quality}), and I can point at the mechanism rather than guess.
Top-$k$ averages only the $\kappa T$ most decisive time steps (\Cref{sec:pool:topk}), so
$\interp_{k,j}$ is exactly zero everywhere the model did not use, instead of a small weight that is
never quite zero. The ablation in \Cref{app:ablations} backs this up directly: NDCG@$n$ is highest
at $\kappa = 0.1$ and falls as $\kappa$ grows towards 1, which is the behaviour the design
predicts.

\paragraph{The best pair was not the pair of the two best halves.}
\texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk} is the best model of the sweep, but neither of its halves is the
best of its kind: the Gated encoder averages 0.902 over its pairings and Top-$k$ pooling averages
0.904, both mid-table (\Cref{sec:res:ablation}). Two ordinary-looking halves making the best whole
is evidence of a real interaction. My reading is that the Gated encoder's channel gate
(\Cref{eq:gate}) already suppresses the channels that carry no signal, and Top-$k$'s hard selection
is the head best placed to use an encoder that has done that cleaning -- a smoother head would blend
back in the neighbours the gate just turned down. I say ``my reading'' on purpose: the grid shows
the effect, but I did not run the one-factor-at-a-time experiment that would prove this mechanism
rather than another one.

\subsection{What did not work}
\label{sec:disc:negative}

\paragraph{Dual-stream pooling: safe on paper, worst in practice.}
This is the clearest negative result of the sweep. Property~\ref{prop:ds} says Dual-stream pooling
can never be worse than the Conjunctive baseline, because its blend $\lambda$ can go to 0 and
recover it exactly. In practice it is the worst head I tried, by a wide margin: mean \webtraffic{}
accuracy 0.744 over its four encoder pairings, against 0.90 and above for Class-wise,
Softmax+temperature and Top-$k$, and one pairing (the Spike/trend encoder) collapses to 0.556.

The safety net is real but nothing exercises it. No term in the loss pushes $\lambda$ towards 0. So
if the critical-instance stream (\Cref{eq:ds_streamB}) learns a bad query early, gradient descent
has no reason to walk back to the safe mean. This is an optimisation failure, not a lack of
capacity or of data -- the model \emph{could} represent the baseline, it just never finds it.

\paragraph{Gated attention: no floor to fall back on.}
Per-class Gated attention is a milder version of the same story. It is the only head in
\Cref{sec:pooling} with no blend parameter, so it cannot reduce to Conjunctive for any setting of
its weights. I flagged that as a structural difference when I designed it, and the results say it
matters: its mean \webtraffic{} accuracy (0.918) is respectable, but it is never the best pairing
for any encoder, unlike Top-$k$ and Class-wise which each produce a top-10 model. Without a
known-good baseline to fall back on, a mis-trained gate has nothing to catch it, so the head lands
in the middle of the table instead of either winning or failing outright.

\paragraph{The lesson from both.}
A mechanism that is safe or expressive \emph{on paper} only pays off if the training recipe
actually reaches the regime where that property matters. Neither of my two weak heads got there
with the single generic recipe I used for the whole sweep (\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}). If I ran
this again, I would either warm-start $\lambda$ near 0 and let the model earn its way up, or add a
small penalty that keeps a blended head close to its safe branch early in training.

\subsection{How the numbers should be read}
\label{sec:disc:reading}

\paragraph{AOPCR is not normalised, and I now have direct proof.}
AOPCR is an area under a curve of raw logit drops, so its scale follows the scale of the model's own
logits, not a fixed 0--1 range like accuracy (\Cref{sec:setup:metrics}). This is a measurement
effect, not a statement about explanation quality, and my own runs show it cleanly.

I trained the \emph{same} architecture twice, changing only the training recipe: once with my recipe
(\texttt{configs/models/sv1/millet.yaml}) and once with \millet{}'s published recipe
(\texttt{sv1/millet\_paper.yaml} -- 1500 epochs, no weight decay, no label smoothing). On
\webtraffic{} the first scores AOPCR 2.57 and the second scores \textbf{13.27}. Same encoder, same
pooling head, same metric code, same dataset: five times the AOPCR, purely from a different training
recipe. That is what an unnormalised metric looks like, and it is exactly why my AOPCR column must
never be compared against an AOPCR printed in another paper -- including \millet{}'s own published
4.55.

\paragraph{A difference smaller than the seed spread is not a result.}
Where I have 3 seeds, the spread is not small. Accuracy standard deviation runs from 0.003
(\texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk}) to 0.030 (\texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive}), and AOPCR is
proportionally much noisier -- 0.437 for \texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive}, about a sixth of its
own mean, and 0.884 for the re-trained \millet{} baseline (\Cref{tab:web_main}).

I used this as a rule, not just as a remark. \texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive}'s AOPCR edge over
\texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} (2.651 against 2.621) is far smaller than either model's own
standard deviation, so I reported it as noise rather than as a win (\Cref{sec:res:explanations}).
The rule cuts both ways: it also stops me claiming that
\texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk}'s accuracy lead over \texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} (0.955
against 0.947) is decisive, because the second model's own spread is 0.016.

For the rest of the sweep I only have one seed, so I cannot apply the same test there. Those
numbers should be read as indicative, not exact.

\paragraph{My training budget is not the published one.}
My recipe trains for at most 400 epochs with patience 60 (\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}). The published
\millet{} numbers come from a recipe about four times longer, with different regularisation. I built
\texttt{sv1/millet\_paper.yaml} to measure exactly this gap by running their recipe on my own
harness. On \webtraffic{} it is already informative -- 0.920 accuracy against 0.887 for my recipe,
so a good part of the gap really is the budget and not the architecture. The full 85-dataset run was
still going when this report was written, so I cannot yet split the \ucr{} gap the same way. Until
it finishes, any comparison against the published column should be read as ``different recipe, not
directly comparable''.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Explanation quality is measured on one dataset.} \webtraffic{} is synthetic, and
        its important regions are generated rather than observed. It is the only dataset in this
        study with per-time-step ground truth, so NDCG@$n$ -- my only metric checked against a real
        answer -- rests on that single dataset. I do not know how it would hold on real, noisier
        traffic.
  \item \textbf{Most models still have one seed.} Four models have 3 seeds on \webtraffic{}, and
        two of them also have 3 seeds over the whole \ucr{} archive. The other 68 have one. Where I
        do have repeats, the spread was large enough to change how I phrase a claim
        (\Cref{sec:disc:reading}), so it is a reason to be careful about the single-seed numbers,
        not a reason to trust them.
  \item \textbf{Only univariate series were tested.} Every dataset here has one channel. The
        encoder does not care how many input channels it starts from, so multivariate input should
        work mechanically, but I have not tested it.
  \item \textbf{My headline models were selected on one dataset.} Both were chosen because they did
        well on \webtraffic{}, and \Cref{sec:res:ucr} shows exactly what that costs: over the 85
        \ucr{} datasets the re-trained baseline beats them both. A choice made on one dataset should
        not be expected to be the best choice on 85 different ones, and it was not.
  \item \textbf{No human study.} AOPCR and NDCG@$n$ both measure whether the model's stated
        explanation matches what the model actually used -- faithfulness, not usefulness. A high
        score does not mean a person would find the highlighted time steps informative. That needs a
        human study, which this internship had no time for.
  \item \textbf{Nothing has run on a real microcontroller yet.} The cost numbers say it should fit
        (\Cref{tab:cost}), but ``should fit'' is not ``does run'' (objective O6,
        \Cref{tab:objectives}).
\end{itemize}
