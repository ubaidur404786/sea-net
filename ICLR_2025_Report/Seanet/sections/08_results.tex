\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

I compare 72 model variants: my \seanet{} encoders paired with my pooling heads and with
\millet{}'s heads, plus the FCN, ResNet and InceptionTime baselines I re-trained myself. Every model
is run on \webtraffic{} and on the \ucr{} archive, so up to 129 datasets per model.

Two rules hold everywhere, so I state them once. First, four models were trained with \textbf{3
seeds} and are marked with a star; every other row is seed~0 only, because 72 variants over 129
datasets was already close to my compute budget (\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}). Second, a number I
produced is never mixed in the same column with a number copied from \citet{early2024millet},
because the two use different recipes.

Four things to notice. First, the whole top ten is mine, and every one of the ten beats the best
baseline at 0.887. Second, the top ten is tight -- 0.946 to 0.955, a range of nine thousandths --
which is why \Cref{sec:disc:reading} refuses to call any of them the winner on accuracy alone.
Third, the parameter column separates what the accuracy column cannot: ranks 2 and 10 need about
269\,K parameters to reach where rank 7 gets with 41\,K. Fourth, and this is the argument of the
whole report in one row, rank 7 has the best NDCG@$n$ of the table (0.773) \emph{and} the smallest
size, so nothing was traded away for the better explanation. That is what Top-$k$ pooling was
designed to do (\Cref{sec:pool:topk}): it averages only the $k$ most decisive time steps, so the
interpretation is exactly zero everywhere the model did not use as evidence, while the baseline's
importance curve is never quite zero and a reader has to guess where the evidence stops
(\Cref{fig:teaser}).

Size is not just a parameter count. Measured on the real \webtraffic{} input, rank 7 is 90\,\%
smaller than the re-trained baseline, its saved file drops from 4.11\,MB to 1.43\,MB, and one
prediction is about 1.6 times faster -- the MB figure being the one that matters for the
microcontroller target of \Cref{sec:context}. The full cost table is \Cref{tab:cost} in the
appendix, and it carries the honest counterpart: my models take 2--3 times \emph{longer to train}.

\Cref{fig:web_bars} puts quality and cost side by side for the five best models, and it is the
clearest single picture of the trade-off: the bars that are longest in the quality panels are among
the shortest in the cost panels.

\label{sec:res:ucr}

datasets \millet{} published. A mean can hide a model that wins hugely on three datasets and loses
everywhere else, so \Cref{tab:ucr_accuracy} reports the mean rank and the per-dataset record next to

This table tells a different story from \Cref{tab:web_main}, and I want to say it plainly instead of
hiding it. Over the \ucr{} archive both \millet{} rows beat all three of my models, which lose more
datasets than they win. That does not cancel the \webtraffic{} result, but it shows its limit: both
headline models were chosen because they did well on \webtraffic{} (\Cref{sec:setup:data}), and one
dataset cannot predict what happens on 85 very different ones. \seanet{} stays competitive -- within
1 to 2 accuracy points of the baseline at about a tenth of the parameters -- but ``better
everywhere'' is a claim this table does not support.

The last two rows are the ones that close objective~O2, and they are worth reading carefully because
they are the \emph{same architecture and the same code}, trained twice under two recipes. Under my
recipe it reaches 0.8274; under \millet{}'s own recipe it reaches 0.8434 against a published 0.8445
-- a gap of about one thousandth, and its per-dataset record against the published numbers is
balanced at 26 wins, 32 ties and 26 losses. So the earlier shortfall was never a bug in my
re-implementation: it was the training budget. That also settles how the rest of this table should
be read. My models are trained under \emph{my} recipe, so the fair comparison for them is the 0.8274
row, not the published one; the gap to 0.8434 measures the recipe, and every model in this report
would move by some part of it under a four-times-longer budget I could not afford for 72 variants
(\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}).

Finally, the gain is not the encoder alone nor the head alone. Averaged over every partner, the
encoder choice moves \webtraffic{} accuracy across a range of 0.858--0.937 and the head choice across
0.744--0.921, two spreads of similar size; and the best pair of all is not built from the two best
halves. \Cref{app:ablations} has the encoder $\times$ head grid and the rest of the ablations.
