\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

In this section I compare 72 model variants. These are my \seanet{} encoders paired with my
pooling heads and with \millet{}'s heads, plus the FCN, ResNet and InceptionTime baselines that
I re-trained myself. Every model is run on \webtraffic{} and on the \ucr{} archive, so up to
129 datasets per model (\Cref{sec:setup:data}).

I say the seed rule once here instead of repeating it in every caption. My four main models --
the two headline \seanet{} variants, the best model of the sweep, and the re-trained \millet{}
baseline -- were trained with \textbf{3 seeds} (0, 1 and 2) on \webtraffic{}, and two of them
also with 3 seeds over the whole \ucr{} archive. So those rows show a real mean $\pm$ standard
deviation. The rest of the sweep is single seed (seed~$=0$), because 72 variants over 129
datasets was already close to my compute budget (\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}). The ResNet and FCN
baselines also have one seed each, so I show no standard deviation for them.

One more rule I follow everywhere: a number I produced myself is never mixed in the same column
with a number copied from \citet{early2024millet}. My numbers use my recipe, theirs use theirs
(\Cref{sec:setup:baselines}), so putting them side by side would hide the difference.

All three of my models beat all three re-trained baselines on all three metrics at the same time,
and they do it with 7 to 10 times fewer parameters. That is the main message of this table.

The second message is that the winner is not the same model for every metric.
\texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk} has the best accuracy (0.955) and it is also the most stable
model in the table: its standard deviation over three seeds is only 0.003.

I pick the first one, and if I want the best explanation for the lowest cost I pick the second.

\texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive} has the highest mean AOPCR (2.651), but I do not read that as
a win. The gap to \texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} (2.621) is much smaller than its own standard

between 0.880 and 0.938 across the three seeds, while
\texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} stays inside 0.938--0.966.

This table tells a different story from \Cref{tab:web_main}, and I want to say that plainly
instead of hiding it. Over the 85 \ucr{} datasets my re-trained \millet{} baseline is the best of
the six models: it has the highest mean accuracy (0.8274) and the best mean rank (11.86). All
three of my models sit around rank 14.6--14.8 and lose more datasets than they win.

I do not think this cancels the \webtraffic{} result, but it does show its limit. Both of my
headline models were chosen because they did well on \webtraffic{}
(\Cref{sec:setup:data}), and one dataset is not enough to predict what happens on 85 very
different ones. \seanet{} is still competitive here -- it stays within 1 to 2 accuracy points of
the baseline while using about a tenth of the parameters -- but ``better everywhere'' is a claim
this table does not support, and I would rather correct it myself than let a reader find it.

Against the published number (0.8445), my re-trained \millet{} baseline lands slightly lower
(0.8274). That is the size of gap a different training recipe can easily produce
(\Cref{sec:disc:reading}), and it is the sanity check \Cref{sec:setup:baselines} was built for.

\subsection{Explanation quality}
\label{sec:res:explanations}

Explanation quality is the point of this internship, so AOPCR and NDCG@$n$ get their own table
instead of being squeezed next to accuracy. One warning first, repeated from
\Cref{sec:setup:metrics}: AOPCR is not normalised, so these values can be compared with each other
but never with a number printed in another paper.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Explanation quality on \webtraffic{} (mean $\pm$ standard deviation over 3 seeds).
AOPCR values are comparable between the rows of this table only.}
\label{tab:explanation_quality}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{AOPCR} & \textbf{NDCG@$n$} & \textbf{Accuracy} \\
\midrule
\texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} (best NDCG)     & $2.621\pm0.214$ & $\mathbf{0.773}\pm0.013$ & $0.947\pm0.016$ \\
\texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive} (best AOPCR) & $\mathbf{2.651}\pm0.437$ & $0.732\pm0.043$ & $0.905\pm0.030$ \\
\texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk} (best accuracy)& $2.225\pm0.131$ & $0.750\pm0.022$ & $\mathbf{0.955}\pm0.003$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{table}
% Same source rows as Table 7; this table only re-orders them and puts the explanation metrics first.

Does a better explanation cost accuracy here? For \texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} the answer is
no. It has the best NDCG@$n$ of the four rows and the second best accuracy, so nothing was traded
away. This is exactly what Top-$k$ pooling was designed to do (\Cref{sec:pool:topk}): it averages
only the $k$ most decisive time steps, so the interpretation $\interp_{k,j}$ is literally zero
everywhere the model did not use as evidence, instead of a small non-zero weight spread over the
whole series.

For \texttt{seanet\_inputgate\_adaptive} there is a real cost. It has the highest mean AOPCR, but
it pays with the lowest accuracy of my three models and the largest spread on every metric. My
reading is that Adaptive class-wise pooling (\Cref{sec:pool:adaptive}) has to \emph{learn} how far
to lean towards a max-like aggregator, through its parameter $\beta_k$. Top-$k$ does not learn
that -- its selection is fixed and hard. One more thing to learn is one more thing that can land
differently on a different seed, and the standard deviations say that is what happens.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{results/SEA_NET/teaser/2026-08-09_15-32-44/teaser_WebTraffic_idx0350}
  \caption{One \webtraffic{} test series explained by three models. Every model sees exactly the
  same input, and all three predict the correct class, so the comparison is only about the
  explanation and not about who was right. Top: a normal black-box model, which gives a class and

The picture matches the numbers. The baseline's importance curve is never exactly zero, so a
reader has to guess where the evidence stops. \seanet{}'s curve is zero almost everywhere and
tall in a few places, which is what a high NDCG@$n$ means in practice: the truly modified time
steps sit at the top of the ranking. I picked this series because all three models classify it
correctly, not because it is the nicest one -- \Cref{app:figures} shows more examples, including
one where the explanation is poor.

The parameter cut is large and it is real. \texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} uses 90\,\% fewer
parameters than the re-trained \millet{} baseline (41{,}324 against 423{,}707, a
10.3$\times$ reduction), 11$\times$ fewer FLOPs, and it is about 1.6$\times$ faster at inference
(0.131\,ms against 0.203\,ms). All three of my models sit between 1.4 and 1.5\,MB, against
4.1\,MB for the baseline, which is the number that matters for a microcontroller
(\Cref{sec:context}).

One honest note about training time. My models take \emph{longer} to train than the baseline
(around 2$\times$), even though they are 10$\times$ smaller and much cheaper per step. That is not
a contradiction: training time here is wall-clock time until early stopping fires, so it measures
how many epochs a model needed, not how expensive one epoch is. The baseline stops earlier because
its validation loss stops improving earlier. FLOPs and inference time are the honest per-step
comparison, and there \seanet{} wins clearly.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:res:ablation}

The main question an ablation must answer here is simple: does the gain come from the encoder,
from the pooling head, or from putting the two together? The rest of my ablations, including the
top-$k$ fraction and the attention focus penalty, are in \Cref{app:ablations}.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{results/paper_figures/02_ablation/ablation_encoder_pooling_grid}
  \caption{Best \webtraffic{} accuracy for every encoder (rows) and every pooling head (columns).
  Read one cell as ``what this pair achieved''; blank cells are pairs I never trained. Rows and
  columns are sorted by their best value, so the strong components move to the top left.}
  \label{fig:ablation_grid}
\end{figure}

Taken on their own, the encoder and the pooling head matter about equally. Averaged over every
head it was paired with, the encoder moves \webtraffic{} accuracy from 0.858 (Spike/trend) up to
0.937 (\texttt{sea\_multiscale\_channels}). Averaged over every encoder it was paired with, the
head moves it from 0.744 (Dual-stream) up to 0.921 (Class-wise conjunctive). Two ranges of a
similar size, so neither half dominates.

The more interesting result is that the best pair is \emph{not} made of the two best halves. The
best model of the whole sweep, \texttt{seanet\_gated\_mean\_topk} (0.955), pairs the Gated encoder
with Top-$k$ pooling. On their own, the Gated encoder averages 0.902 and Top-$k$ pooling averages
0.904 -- both are mid-table, neither is the best of its kind. So the gain is not simply the sum of
two good parts, and I could only have found this pair by trying the combination.

One caveat I want to keep visible: several groups in these averages contain very few models (as
few as one), and not every encoder $\times$ head pair was trained. So the marginal averages are a
coarse summary, and the grid itself, read cell by cell, is the more trustworthy view.
