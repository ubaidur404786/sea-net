\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

I compare 72 model variants: my \seanet{} encoders paired with my pooling heads and with
\millet{}'s heads, plus the FCN, ResNet and InceptionTime baselines I re-trained myself. Every model
is run on \webtraffic{} and on the \ucr{} archive, so up to 129 datasets per model.

Two rules hold everywhere, so I state them once. First, four models were trained with \textbf{3
seeds} and are marked with a star; every other row is seed~0 only, because 72 variants over 129
datasets was already close to my compute budget (\Cref{sec:setup:protocol}). Second, a number I
produced is never mixed in the same column with a number copied from \citet{early2024millet},
because the two use different recipes.

Three things to notice. First, the whole top ten is mine, and the best baseline sits at 0.887, so
every one of the ten beats it. Second, the top ten is tight -- 0.946 to 0.955, a range of nine
thousandths -- which is why \Cref{sec:disc:reading} refuses to call any of them the winner on
accuracy alone. Third, the parameter column separates what the accuracy column cannot: ranks 2 and
10 need about 269\,K parameters to reach the same place that rank 7 reaches with 41\,K.

That is the argument of this report in one row. Rank 7 has the best NDCG@$n$ of the table (0.773)
\emph{and} the smallest size, so nothing was traded away for the better explanation. This is what
Top-$k$ pooling was designed to do (\Cref{sec:pool:topk}): it averages only the $k$ most decisive
time steps, so the interpretation is exactly zero everywhere the model did not use as evidence.
\Cref{fig:teaser} shows what that looks like on one series -- the baseline's importance curve is
never quite zero, so a reader has to guess where the evidence stops.

This table tells a different story from \Cref{tab:web_main}, and I want to say it plainly instead of
hiding it. Over the 85 \ucr{} datasets my re-trained \millet{} baseline is the best of the six, and
all three of my models lose more datasets than they win. That does not cancel the \webtraffic{}
result, but it shows its limit: both headline models were chosen because they did well on
\webtraffic{} (\Cref{sec:setup:data}), and one dataset cannot predict what happens on 85 very
different ones. \seanet{} is still competitive -- within 1 to 2 accuracy points of the baseline at
about a tenth of the parameters -- but ``better everywhere'' is a claim this table does not support.
Against the published number my re-trained baseline lands slightly lower, which is the size of gap a
different training recipe can easily produce (\Cref{sec:disc:reading}).

The size cut is large and it is real: 41{,}324 parameters against 423{,}707 is 90\,\% fewer, the
saved file drops from 4.11\,MB to 1.43\,MB, and one prediction is about 1.6 times faster. The MB
column is the one that matters for a microcontroller (\Cref{sec:context}).

The last column is the honest counterweight. My models take \emph{longer} to train than the
baseline, roughly 2 to 3 times, on every seed. That is not a contradiction: training time here is
wall-clock time until early stopping fires, so it measures how many epochs a model needed, not how
expensive one epoch is. The baseline simply stops improving earlier. Prediction time is the fair
per-step comparison, and there \seanet{} wins.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:res:ablation}

The question here is simple: does the gain come from the encoder, from the pooling head, or from
putting the two together? The rest of my ablations, including the top-$k$ fraction and the attention
focus penalty, are in \Cref{app:ablations}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{results/paper_figures/02_ablation/ablation_encoder_pooling_grid}
  \caption{Best \webtraffic{} accuracy for every encoder (rows) and every pooling head (columns).
  Read one cell as ``what this pair achieved''; blank cells are pairs I never trained. Rows and
  columns are sorted by their best value, so the strong components move to the top left.}
  \label{fig:ablation_grid}
\end{figure}

Taken on their own, the encoder and the head matter about equally: averaged over every head it was
paired with, the encoder moves accuracy from 0.858 to 0.937, and averaged over every encoder, the
head moves it from 0.744 to 0.921 (\Cref{tab:ablation_pooling}, \Cref{tab:ablation_encoder}). Two
ranges of a similar size, so neither half dominates. The more interesting result is that the best
pair is \emph{not} made of the two best halves: the winner of \Cref{tab:web_main} pairs the Gated
encoder, which averages 0.902, with Top-$k$ pooling, which averages 0.904 -- both mid-table. The
gain is not the sum of two good parts, and I could only have found this pair by trying the
combination. One caveat to keep visible: several groups in these averages contain very few models,
and not every pair was trained, so the grid read cell by cell is the more trustworthy view.
