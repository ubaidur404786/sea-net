The last column is the honest counterweight. My models take \emph{longer} to train than the
baseline, roughly 2 to 3 times, on every seed. That is not a contradiction: training time here is

expensive one epoch is. The baseline simply stops improving earlier. Prediction time is the fair
per-step comparison, and there \seanet{} wins.

Two things come out of this. First, selecting a subset really does help:
$\kappa = 1.0$, which is exactly Class-wise conjunctive pooling, scores 0.908, clearly below the
0.938--0.940 of $\kappa = 0.1$ and $\kappa = 0.25$. So the gain of Top-$k$ is not an accident of
the encoder, it comes from the selection itself. Second, NDCG@$n$ behaves the way the design
predicts: it is highest at $\kappa = 0.1$ (0.777) and falls steadily as $\kappa$ grows towards 1
(0.722). Keeping fewer time steps really does put the truly modified ones nearer the top of the
ranking, which is the claim \Cref{sec:pool:topk} makes.

Being too aggressive hurts, though: at $\kappa = 0.05$ accuracy drops to 0.888. So there is a real
middle ground, and the 0.1 I picked by hand before running this sweep turns out to sit in it.

The result is a clean trade, and not the one I expected. Turning the penalty \emph{off} gives
better classification (0.950 against 0.938, and a lower loss), but worse explanations: AOPCR drops
from 2.778 to 2.303 and NDCG@$n$ from 0.777 to 0.765. So the penalty does what it was designed to
do -- it forces the attention onto fewer time steps -- and it pays for that with about one point of
accuracy. Since this report is about interpretability, keeping it on is the right default, but a
reader who only wants accuracy should turn it off. This is a single pair on a single seed, so I
present it as a direction, not as a measured effect size.

accuracy over the 85 \ucr{} datasets, against 0.8237 and 0.8253 for the two models alone
(\Cref{tab:ensemble}) -- about one accuracy point. Soft voting beats hard voting (0.8335 against
0.8311), which fits the idea that averaging the full probabilities keeps more information than
counting arg-max votes.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter search.}
For the base \seanet{} recipe I ran a 30-trial Optuna search \citep{akiba2019optuna} with a TPE
sampler over nine hyperparameters at once: learning rate, weight decay, label smoothing and the
attention entropy penalty from the training recipe, plus the encoder width, depth, dropout and
dilation cap, plus the pooling head's attention width. Each trial trained on \webtraffic{} and
Optuna minimised the validation loss.

It did not beat my hand-set default. The best trial reached 0.906 test accuracy against 0.942 for
the default recipe. I read this as a useful negative result rather than a failed search: the
default was built up by hand, one change at a time, while I was designing the architecture in
\Cref{sec:architecture}, so it was probably already near a local optimum for this dataset -- at
least inside the ranges I gave Optuna to search.
% Source: configs/models/sv2/seanet.yaml, records.optuna_best.metrics.test_acc = 0.906 (30 trials)
% against records.default.metrics.test_acc = 0.942. Both on WebTraffic per the recorded metadata.

\paragraph{An approach I scoped and dropped.}
I planned a Transformer encoder (\texttt{configs/models/transformer.yaml}: 128-d model, 8 heads,
4 layers, reusing \millet{}'s Additive head) but never trained it. I want to be precise: the config
exists with draft hyperparameters and an explicit \texttt{implemented: false} flag, so the pipeline
refuses to run it until the encoder class is written, and I never wrote that class. I set it aside
because the TCN-family encoders already delivered the parameter saving objective O3 asked for, and
a Transformer's attention cost grows with the square of the series length -- the wrong direction for
Goal~G1 -- before it could show whether it helps explanation quality at all. It stays a reasonable
next step for a future iteration that cares more about accuracy than size.

\paragraph{Accuracy against series length.}
One question the design raises directly: does the capped dilation hurt on long series? I checked it
on \texttt{seanet\_bottleneck\_topk} over all 128 \ucr{} datasets, averaged over its 3 seeds. The
correlation between accuracy and series length is $-0.34$. Splitting the archive at the median
length (344 time steps), the shorter half averages 0.857 accuracy and the longer half 0.755 -- a
gap of about 10 points.

I read this as a real limitation of the design rather than noise, and it is consistent with the
mechanism: \texttt{max\_dilation} is 16 in every \seanet{} config (\Cref{sec:arch:block}), so the
receptive field stops growing after a fixed reach. On a series much longer than that reach, the
later blocks work from an increasingly incomplete view. The effect size (10 points) is far larger
than any seed-to-seed spread I measured (\Cref{tab:seeds}). Raising the dilation cap for long
datasets is the obvious thing to try next, and \Cref{sec:conclusion} lists it as future work.
