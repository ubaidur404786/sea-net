\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:disc}

\paragraph{The trade-off.}
\Cref{sec:results} in one sentence: ten times fewer parameters and ten times less computation, in
exchange for about two accuracy points on a large archive, with the interpretation better rather than
worse where we can score it. Whether that is a good trade depends on where the model runs, not on the
model itself. On a workstation it is a bad trade. On a sensor node with a megabyte of flash it is the
difference between a method that can be used and one that cannot, and the interpretation, which is
the reason to use MIL at all, is not lost. Since the wide encoder wins back the \ucr{} points
(\Cref{sec:res:ucr}), the price is paid for the smaller size, and it can be changed by choosing a
different width. The wider point: the tension between ``interpretable'' and ``small'' was never part
of MIL --- its head costs $0.25\,\%$ of the baseline --- but came from a backbone chosen when cost was
not a design choice.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\textbf{No deployment}: we report parameter counts, FLOPs counted by hand from the layer shapes and
GPU timings; no model was quantised, compiled or run on a microcontroller, and the int8 column of
\Cref{tab:cost} is a calculation, not a measurement. \textbf{Activation memory did not shrink}: peak
memory is worse than the baseline's (\Cref{sec:res:cost}) because the encoder keeps the length
unchanged by design, so where SRAM and not flash is the limit, this is the wrong design; versions
that stream the input or use strides while keeping a per-step interpretation are the obvious next
step. \textbf{Interpretation is scored on one dataset}: \ndcg{} needs per-time-step ground truth,
which only the synthetic \webtraffic{} generator gives, and that is also the set we chose the design
on --- so the interpretation claim rests on one optimistic benchmark, with AOPCR as the weaker backup
(\Cref{sec:res:aopcr}). \textbf{Uneven seeds}: three models have three seeds and the ablations use one
seed each, read as a pattern against the spreads in \Cref{sec:setup}; running all 72 combinations with
several seeds needed more compute than we had. \textbf{Training is slower}: $117.3 \pm 14.0$\,s to
converge on \webtraffic{} against $50.4 \pm 9.5$\,s for the baseline, because GPU kernels are tuned
for dense convolutions and not for depthwise ones. The saving is at inference time, where the target
device spends its life, but the training cost is real.
