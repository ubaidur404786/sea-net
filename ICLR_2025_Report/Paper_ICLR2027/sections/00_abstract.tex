% ABSTRACT -- four questions, in order: problem / limitation / method /
% result. No implementation detail, no adjectives, no claim that is not
% in Section 5.
% =====================================================================

Time series classifiers on sensor nodes are often asked for two things at once: a prediction, and a
sign of \emph{which part of the signal caused it}. Multiple instance learning gives both from a
single forward pass, and \millet{} \citep{early2024millet} is the standard method for time series.
Its cost, however, comes from an InceptionTime backbone: 424\,K parameters and 848\,MFLOPs per
series, ten to a hundred times more than the memory and compute available on microcontroller-class
hardware. We ask whether \emph{classification $+$ interpretation} still works when the model is made
ten times smaller. We replace the backbone with a length-preserving multi-scale depthwise-separable
dilated convolutional encoder, and split its pointwise channel mixing --- the step that holds most
of its parameters --- into two smaller matrices through a narrow bottleneck. We also replace the
shared-gate mean of conjunctive pooling with a per-class \mbox{Top-$k$} head that averages only the
$\kappa$ fraction of time steps that support the class most. Neither part is new on its own; our
contribution is the combination and the measurement of what it costs. The resulting model has
\textbf{41.3\,K parameters} and \textbf{76.7\,MFLOPs} per series, $10.3\times$ and $11.1\times$ below
the baseline. On \webtraffic{}, the only dataset here with per-time-step ground truth, it beats the
equally-trained baseline at both jobs at once: accuracy $0.947$ against $0.887$, \ndcg{} $0.772$
against $0.677$. Across 85 \ucr{} datasets it loses $1.8$ accuracy points against that baseline and
$3.4$ against \millet{}'s published recipe, and a wider version of the same encoder wins those points
back --- so the loss comes from the smaller size, not from the design. An ablation separates the two
parts: the bottleneck removes 28\,\% of the parameters with no accuracy cost we can measure, and
\mbox{Top-$k$} pooling gives the better interpretation. We also show that AOPCR, the faithfulness
measure used in this field, changes by $5.2\times$ on a fixed architecture when only the training
budget changes, so faithfulness can only be compared between models trained the same way. We report a
reduction in parameters and FLOPs, not a model running on a device.
