\section{Metric Definitions}
\label{app:metrics}

\paragraph{AOPCR.}
Let $\bagpred^{\,c}(\bag)$ be the logit of the predicted class $c$ on the unchanged series, and let
$\bag^{(m)}$ be the series with the $m$ time steps that the model itself ranks most important for $c$
replaced by the mean of that series. AOPCR adds up the drop over this order,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{AOPCR} = \frac{1}{M+1}\sum_{m=0}^{M}
      \Big( \bagpred^{\,c}(\bag) - \bagpred^{\,c}\big(\bag^{(m)}\big) \Big),
  \label{eq:aopc}
\end{equation}
following \citet{samek2017aopc} as adapted to time series by \citet{early2024millet}, with $M$ set to
$10\,\%$ of the series length in steps of $1\,\%$. Higher is better. Note that \Cref{eq:aopc} is a
difference of logits, and logits have \emph{no fixed scale}. This is the cause of the comparison
problem described in \Cref{sec:res:aopcr}: two models whose logits sit on different scales give AOPCR
values on different scales, however good or bad their rankings are.

\paragraph{\ndcg{}.}
Where per-time-step ground truth exists, we instead rank the $\nsteps$ time steps by the importance
$\interp_{c,j}$ the model claims, and score that ranking with normalised discounted cumulative gain
\citep{jarvelin2002ndcg} at cut-off $n$, the number of steps the generator actually marked as
carrying the class signal. A step is either relevant or not. The score is divided by the score of the
best possible ranking of the same series, so \ndcg{} lies in $[0,1]$ and can be compared between
models, datasets and papers --- which is why the interpretation claims of this paper rest on it and
not on AOPCR.

\section{Model and Training Details}

\subsection{Encoder Variants}
\label{app:encoders}

The main text uses two encoders: the plain multi-scale separable stack of \Cref{sec:method:enc}
(\texttt{sea\_mstcn\_sep}) and its bottleneck form (\Cref{eq:bottleneck},
\texttt{sea\_mstcn\_sep\_bottleneck}). We also ran five other variants, shown in
\Cref{tab:app_encoders}: \emph{gated}, which turns the time axis into one summary vector and uses it
to re-weight the channels; \emph{input-gated}, which reads the same gate from the raw input instead;
\emph{spike/trend}, which runs a smoothed branch and a high-pass branch and mixes them;
\emph{reconstruction-residual}, which adds features built from a reconstruction error; and two
\emph{wrappers} that do not replace an encoder but sit around one --- \emph{multi-scale channels},
which adds rolling max/min/std input channels, and \emph{multi-scale pyramid}, which runs the same
encoder at four downsampling rates and joins them back to length $\nsteps$. All of them keep
$\nsteps$ unchanged. Only the bottleneck is part of this paper's contribution; the others are
background showing that the bottleneck was the right place to cut.

\subsection{Pooling Heads}
\label{app:heads}

Every head keeps the two-branch form of \Cref{eq:conj_gate}, so a head is fully described by two
choices: the shape of the attention branch, and the operation that turns the $\nsteps$ evidence
values $g_j^k$ into one number. \Cref{tab:app_heads} lists all seven by those two choices, together
with the setting at which each one becomes conjunctive pooling exactly. Two come from histopathology
MIL rather than from us: gated attention is due to \citet{ilse2018attention} and the dual-stream
mechanism to \citet{li2021dsmil}; our changes to those two are the move to time series, the per-class
form, and --- for the dual stream --- dropping its contrastive pre-training and adding a learnable
mix.

\Cref{tab:app_pooling} averages each head over every encoder it was paired with. Two things here
support statements in the main text. First, the spread caused by the head choice ($0.744$ to $0.921$)
is as large as the spread caused by the encoder choice in \Cref{tab:app_encoders} ($0.771$ to
$0.937$), so the pooling head is a main design choice and not a detail. Second, being able to return
to the baseline is \emph{not} a safety guarantee: five of the seven heads contain conjunctive pooling
as a special case, and the dual-stream head, which is one of them, is still the worst head we tested
--- its four pairings score $0.860$, $0.824$, $0.736$ and $0.556$. The reason is visible in the
training runs: its mixing weight starts at $\lambda_0 = 0.05$ and nothing in \Cref{eq:loss} pushes it
back towards the baseline setting, so being \emph{able} to return to the baseline does not mean
training will move it there. That is why the main text argues for \mbox{Top-$k$} using measured
\ndcg{} and not only this property.

\subsection{Hyperparameters}
\label{app:hparams}

The same for every model and every dataset unless stated: Adam \citep{kingma2015adam}, learning rate
$1.25\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $1.2\times10^{-4}$, at most 400 epochs, early stopping patience 60
on validation loss, batch size $\min(\lfloor n_{\mathrm{train}}/10 \rfloor, 16)$ with a floor of 2,
label smoothing $0.13$, stratified $80/20$ validation split when $n_{\mathrm{train}} \ge 100$ and
training-set monitoring otherwise. Encoder: $\dmodel = 64$, 4 blocks (wide: $\dmodel = 128$, 6
blocks), kernels $\{5, 11, 23\}$, dilation doubling per block capped at 16, dropout $0.2$. Head:
attention width 8, $\kappa = 0.1$, $\tau_0 = 1.0$, $\beta_0 = 0.3$, $\lambda_0 = 0.5$
(attention-max) or $0.05$ (dual-stream). Loss: $\lambda_{\mathrm{focus}} = 0.01$ for our models and
$0$ for the \millet{} baselines, since the term is our addition. The published-recipe baseline uses
\millet{}'s own settings instead, where the difference that matters is 1{,}500 epochs against our
400. \webtraffic{} is produced at the released defaults: 1{,}000 series, length 1{,}008, 10 classes,
$50/50$ train/test split. Each series is standardised on its own; nothing else is done to the data.

\Cref{tab:app_ucr} is the evidence behind \Cref{sec:res:ucr}. Two comparisons are worth separating.
Keeping the training budget fixed and changing the backbone at a similar size moves the mean rank
from $12.60$ to $11.53$, about $1.1$ places. Keeping the architecture fixed and changing only the
training budget moves it from $12.60$ to $9.41$, about $3.2$ places --- three times as much. Any
comparison of backbones in this field that does not keep the budget fixed is mostly measuring the
budget.

A Wilcoxon--Holm test over the same datasets finds that most of the differences in the middle of this
table are not significant at $\alpha = 0.05$. That is one more reason the main text argues from
\webtraffic{} and from cost rather than from \ucr{} rank, and a reason we give the win/tie/loss counts
above instead of relying on the rank order alone.

go against our own finding.
